A simulation you can run

Lucky

Give a thousand people different talents and the same start. Roll good luck and bad luck across their lives for forty years. Who ends up on top?

After Pluchino, Biondo & Rapisarda, “Talent vs Luck”, Advances in Complex Systems, 2018 · Ig Nobel Prize in Economics, 2022

Live: people are ink dots, sized by their wealth

01 · The world

Four rules and a coin

A thousand people

Scatter 1,000 people across a square world of 201 by 201 patches. Its edges wrap around, so nobody lives at the border. They never move.

Talent is a bell curve

Each person gets a fixed talent between 0 and 1: intelligence, skill, grit, whatever helps you grab a chance. It follows a normal distribution, averaging 0.6 with a spread of 0.1. Most people sit near the middle; a few are brilliant.

Everyone starts equal

Each person starts with 10 units of capital, the model’s stand-in for success or wealth. Nobody gets a head start.

Then the balls

Release 500 events: half lucky, half unlucky. Every six months each one rolls two patches in a random direction. A good idea, a chance meeting, a lucky break; an illness, a crash, a layoff.

Green: talent decides

When a green ball passes within one patch of you, you flip a weighted coin. With talent 0.62, you have a 62% chance to seize it. Seize it and your capital doubles. Miss it and nothing happens.

Red: talent doesn’t help

When a red ball reaches you, your capital halves. No coin, no talent check. A car crash doesn’t care how clever you are.

Forty years

Run it for 80 steps of six months: a working life from 20 to 60. That’s the whole model. Now you run it.

05 · Where you’re born

More green balls

You can’t pick your luck, but a country, a city or a family can tilt the mix. Change the share of lucky events and count how many of the most talented people get ahead.

“Talented” here means one spread above average: talent over 0.7, about one person in six. The question is how many of them finish with more than the 10 units they started with.

06 · Handing out grants

Reward the winners?

The authors asked a practical question: if you had money to hand out every five years, say research funding, how should you split it so the most talented people actually get ahead?

Same budget each time, 10,000 units over forty years (1,250 every five years), split four ways:

  • Equal: everyone gets 1.25 units.
  • To the best: the richest 25% get 5 units each. This is “rewarding success”.
  • Mixed: half to the richest 25%, half shared by everyone else.
  • Lottery: 25% of people, drawn at random, get 5 units each.

07 · What it doesn’t prove

A toy, honestly read

The model is deliberately tiny. It shows that luck can outweigh talent under simple rules, not that it does in your life.

Doubling does the work

Success multiplies: double or halve. Multiplicative games always end lopsided, even with no talent at all. Make luck add or subtract a fixed amount instead and the extremes shrink.

Luck ignores wealth

Real opportunities find the already successful: connections, capital, attention. Here a green ball is as likely to hit the poor as the rich. Real inequality is probably steeper than this.

Talent is frozen

Nobody learns, moves, or seeks out green balls. In life, talent includes going where the chances are.

Talent still matters

Talented people do better on average, and the top performers lean above average. Talent is necessary but not sufficient, which is the paper’s own conclusion.

So, merit?

If success were mostly talent, the richest person in each world would be the most gifted. In this model they almost never are: they are usually average-plus people who caught a streak of green. The authors call rewarding past success “naive meritocracy”, because it ends up rewarding the lucky and calling it talent. Their alternative is less glamorous: spread the chances wide, and let talent meet more green balls.

The simulation re-implements the model in JavaScript (js/sim.js), following the paper and, where the text leaves details open, the authors’ NetLogo code (CoMSES Model Library): a person meets at most one event per six months, and each event is lucky with 50% probability. Over 2,000 runs it gives a richest-person talent of 0.669 ± 0.094 (paper: 0.667 ± 0.09), about 77% of wealth held by the top 20%, and about 31% of talented people ending ahead (paper: 32%). The paper’s single example run (top 20% holding 80%, richest 2,560 units) sits well inside the spread of this page’s runs.
