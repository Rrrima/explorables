About this report

Each claim carries a source tag such as H4, M2 or R8. The letter names the chapter's source list: H for history and culture, M for material and texture, R for rendering. Three markers show confidence:

  • Disputed scholars disagree, or the claim has been revised.
  • Unverified widely repeated, but no source was found.
  • Derived calculated here from sourced numbers, or a suggestion rather than a sourced value.

Several museum and university pages blocked direct reading, so some fibre figures come from search extracts of those pages. They are marked where it matters. Coordinates for the map of the spread are approximate city centres.

Chapter H

History & culture

H1 · Origins: the Cai Lun story and the archaeology

The Hou Hanshu, compiled in the 5th century, records that the court eunuch Cai Lun presented paper to Emperor He in 105 CE, made from tree bark, hemp remnants, rags and fishing nets H1 H2. The wording comes from an English paraphrase; it has not been checked against the Chinese text here. Cai Lun is now generally credited with standardising and promoting papermaking, not inventing it H4 H5.

H3 · Traditions

Japanese washi

Three bast fibres: kōzo (long and strong, for shoji and calligraphy), mitsumata (fine and glossy, for banknotes) and gampi (fine and lustrous, for sutras) H26. Papermaking was winter work for farming families H27. Nagashizuki builds a thin, strong sheet by repeated scoops in a bath thickened with neri, a mucilage from tororo-aoi root that slows drainage; tamezuki is a single dip without neri H26 H28. Shinto shide, the zigzag paper streamers, mark sacred space and descend from bark-fibre thread mentioned in the Kojiki H30 H31. Bank of Japan notes blend mitsumata and abacá; over 90% of mitsumata is now imported H32 H33.

UNESCO inscribed "Washi, craftsmanship of traditional Japanese hand-made paper" in 2014, covering Sekishu-banshi, Hon-minoshi and Hosokawa-shi, all made only from kōzo H29 H34 H35. In December 2025 the listing was extended to Echizen Torinoko-shi H36 H37.

Chinese Xuan paper

Made in Jing County, Anhui, from the bark of Pteroceltis tatarinowii and sandy-field rice straw, by hand in more than 100 steps over more than two years H39 H40 H41. Raw shengxuan is unsized and absorbent, so ink blooms; shuxuan is sized with alum and glue and holds a fine line H42 H43. UNESCO Representative List, 2009 H39. In 2018 the main producer made about 600 t a year, roughly 80% of Jingxian output, and only five makers were certified for genuine Xuan H41. Figures vary with how the sector is defined H44.

Korean hanji

Inner bark of dak (paper mulberry). Webal-tteugi is a single-mould, side-to-side scoop native to Korea that crosses the fibres in many directions; dochim beats the damp sheets to compact them and reduce bleeding H45 H46. Nominated to UNESCO in March 2024; the decision is expected in December 2026 H47 H48 H49.

Lokta, deh-sho, saa, khadi, amate

Nepali lokta comes from Daphne bark, cooked in lye and beaten; it nearly died out by the 1950s and revived after 1969 H15. Bhutanese deh-sho is used for prayer texts H50. Thai saa is made from paper mulberry at Ban Ton Pao H51. India's Kumarappa National Handmade Paper Institute researches about 40 waste fibres H52. Otomí amate from San Pablito is boiled bark laid in a grid and pounded with a stone; curers cut it into spirit figures H53 H54.

European rag paper

Fabriano adapted water-powered fulling mills to pound rags, replaced starch with gelatin sizing and made the first watermarks, dated 1282 H19 H20 H55 H56. Laid paper shows chain lines and laid lines from the wire mould. Baskerville's 1757 Virgil contains the first Western wove paper, almost certainly James Whatman's H57 H58; Disputed Cathleen Baker argues it was formed with cloth over a laid mould. The Hollander beater, c. 1680, did in a day what stampers did in eight, at the cost of shorter fibres H59. Of 160–185 Gutenberg Bibles, about three-quarters were printed on paper; 49 survive H60 H61.

Machines, wood and acid

Nicolas-Louis Robert patented a paper machine in 1799; the Fourdrinier brothers' patent followed in 1806–07 H62 H63. Keller and Fenerty made groundwood pulp in the early 1840s; sulfite (1866–74) and kraft (1879–84) followed H4 H64 H65. Alum-rosin sizing and lignin left much post-1850 paper acidic and brittle within a lifetime H66. ISO 9706 defines permanent paper by tear strength, alkali reserve, low lignin and pH H67; its current status differs between catalogue pages.

H4 · Cultural details

Song-dynasty China issued the first state paper money, the jiaozi, in 1024 H68 H69 H70. Paper replaced coins and silk as grave goods in the Wei–Jin period and joss paper was common under the Tang H71 H72; "2,500 years old" is unsupported. The Diamond Sutra of 868 is the earliest dated printed book H4. The Tiangong Kaiwu of 1637 illustrates each step of papermaking H4.

Chapter M

Material & texture

M1 · What paper is

Paper is a felted network of plant cells, mostly cellulose: chains of glucose packed into microfibrils about 3–3.7 nm wide M31 M32 M33. Wood also carries hemicellulose and 17–35% lignin; scoured cotton is about 99% cellulose M30 M31. Sheet strength falls in strongly hydrogen-bonding solvents, the classic evidence that hydrogen bonds at fibre crossings carry load, though not necessarily alone M35. Beating swells the fibre wall so fibres collapse into ribbons and conform; drying "hornifies" them, which is why recycled fibre bonds less well M34 M36.

A sheet is mostly air. With solid cellulose at about 1.5 g/cm³, handsheets of 0.44–0.71 g/cm³ are 55–72% void M52. Derived 80 gsm copy paper at 0.73 g/cm³ is about half air; tengujō at about 0.08 g/cm³ would be over 90% air.

Paper yellows and embrittles through acid hydrolysis and oxidation, mainly in the amorphous regions of cellulose M37 M39. Lignin chromophores absorb near-UV and photo-oxidise to yellow compounds M37; cellulose itself generates acids as it ages, with oxalic acid a main contributor M38 M40, and alum sizing may matter more than lignin M39.

Laid paper carries chain lines 22–32 mm apart and 7–10 laid lines per centimetre M63 M64. Laid-line density fluctuates in a pattern unique to each mould, which lets scholars match "moldmates" M65. A deckle edge is genuine on handmade and mould-made sheets and imitated on machine paper M66. Gelatin sizing arrived at Fabriano in the 13th century; rosin-alum from about 1807 needed pH below 5; alkaline AKD and ASA sizing, with calcium carbonate, became standard in the US in the 1980s M41 M42 M43. Machines align fibres in the machine direction, so tensile strength peaks along it and tear across it M67.

M4 · How texture forms

Long fibres tangle into flocs. The crowding number N = ⅔·Cv·(L/d)² counts fibres within one fibre length; formation worsens sharply above about 16 and flocs persist above about 60 M68. Because N grows with (L/d)², kozo is about fifty times more prone to flocking than eucalyptus at the same concentration Derived. Neri keeps those fibres dispersed and slows drainage, which is what makes nagashizuki possible M73. Calendering compresses the sheet for smoothness and gloss M70.

Opacity comes from light scattering at unbonded fibre–air surfaces M71 M84. A washi sheet only a few long fibres thick, with no filler, has few such surfaces, so light passes through; glassine reaches translucency the opposite way, by beating and calendering the air out M72. Synthesis no single paper quantifies the washi case.

M5 · Ink and paper

Liquid wicks into paper roughly as the Lucas–Washburn law, L = √(γ·r·cos θ·t / 2η): depth grows with the square root of time M76. Sizing raises the contact angle θ; as θ nears 90°, cos θ nears zero and wicking stops. Unsized shengxuan blooms; rice straw's short fibres soften the edges of the bleed M77 M78. Sumi is soot of 15–400 nm bound with animal glue, and inert M80. Iron gall ink is acidic (pH 1–3) and catalyses Fenton oxidation, eating lace-like holes along old strokes M81. Brightness is reflectance in blue light at 457 nm, where yellowing absorbs M82; Kubelka–Munk theory models a sheet by its scattering s and absorption k M83.

M6 · Microstructure

Pores range from tens of nanometres to tens of microns M53 M54; one SEM study measured surface pores of about 8 µm between fibres about 14 µm wide M55. Eucalyptus kraft holds about 20 million fibres per gram, loblolly pine about 3.8 million M56 M57 M58. Derived an 80 gsm A4 sheet weighs about 5 g, so an all-eucalyptus sheet holds about 100 million fibres.

Chapter R

Rendering paper

R1 · Procedural noise

Perlin's 1985 "An Image Synthesizer" introduced lattice gradient noise with a narrow frequency band and the turbulence sum Σ|noise(2kp)|/2k R1. "Improving Noise" (2002) replaced the cubic fade 3t²−2t³ with 6t⁵−15t⁴+10t³ and used twelve edge gradients R2 R3. Simplex noise uses N+1 corners and radial kernels R4. Fractal Brownian motion sums octaves: fbm(p) = Σ Gk·noise(λkp), usually λ = 2, G = ½ R5. Worley noise measures distance to the nearest feature points; F₂−F₁ draws cell borders R7. Domain warping evaluates f(p + h(p)) and turns clouds into swirls R6.

R2 · SVG filter primitives

feTurbulence implements Perlin turbulence with the Park–Miller generator and still uses the old cubic curve; fractalNoise sums signed noise, turbulence sums absolute noise, and stitchTiles makes seamless tiles R8 R9. Filters default to linearRGB, so set color-interpolation-filters="sRGB" for predictable values. feDiffuseLighting reads height from the alpha channel; feeding opaque noise gives flat lighting R8.

WebKit rendered filters in viewport pixels on retina screens, and removed the filterRes workaround in 2018 R10–R13. Current status untested. CSS mask-image with a noise tile avoids the problem and is what this essay uses R14.

R3 · From height to light

Normals come from height differences, N = normalize(−s·∂h/∂x, −s·∂h/∂y, 1); Sobel kernels smooth the estimate and are what feDiffuseLighting uses R8 R17. Lambert shading is k·max(0, N·L) R18. Conservators use raking light, a single source nearly parallel to the sheet, to reveal laid structure, chain lines, watermarks and creases, lighting from several directions because features parallel to the beam vanish R19 R20. Reflectance Transformation Imaging generalises this to many angles R21.

R4 · Modelling paper

Curtis et al. (1997) model paper as a height field and a fluid-capacity field generated from Perlin or Worley noise, simulate shallow water, pigment deposition and capillary flow, darken edges, and composite glazes with Kubelka–Munk R22. Guo and Kunii (1991) model rice paper as randomly distributed fibres through which ink diffuses R23 R24. Lee (2001) divides paper into "papels" with fibre structure in two directions R25. Chu and Tai's MoXi (2005) uses a lattice Boltzmann model for ink percolating through paper at interactive rates R26 R27. Paper tearing has been simulated with hybrid geometric–physical methods R28 R29 R30 R31. No dedicated paper on fibre-level paper appearance was found.

Formation is the distribution of fibres in the sheet; poor formation looks cloudy against the light R33. Chain lines (about 25–35 mm) and laid lines (about 1 mm) are thinner zones that show in transmitted light, as do watermarks R34 R19.

R5 · Ink in the browser

A cellular automaton with water and pigment per cell, conductivity raised along fibres and lowered by sizing, reproduces feathered bleeding at 256² in plain JavaScript R22 R24. Edge darkening can follow Curtis, p ← p − η(1 − blur(M))·M with η 0.01–0.05 R22. Lattice Boltzmann needs WebGL2 float textures R27.

R6 · Recipes

Suggested starting points

  • Washi: formation noise at 3 octaves; 1,500–3,000 long curved fibres per 1000² with log-normal lengths; translucency drives look-through.
  • Cold press: inverted, blurred Worley bumps plus fine fractal noise; light at 40°.
  • Laid: laid period about 1 mm, chain lines about 25 mm with jitter, watermark as thinner paper, shown with exp(−k·thickness) in transmitted light.
  • Newsprint: 40–50 gsm groundwood, yellowing with light; speckle and show-through R38.
  • Performance: bake textures once into a canvas at device pixel ratio and reuse them; never animate feTurbulence or filter scroll-linked elements R15 R16.
