Fibre by Fibre

A sheet of paper is a mat of plant cells, mostly air, held together by water's memory. It took a thousand years to cross Asia, and its surface still records the mould it was lifted from.

This essay follows paper from the fibre to the screen: what it is, where it travelled, why light and ink behave as they do on its surface, and how to rebuild that surface in code. Every number links to the research behind it.

II · Journey

Westward, slowly

200BCE

The traditional story credits a court official, Cai Lun, who presented paper to the Han emperor in 105 CE. The ground says paper is older: fragments from the 1st century BCE, and a contested map from Fangmatan that may be a century older still H1.

Paper moved east to Korea and Japan within centuries. Westward it waited at Dunhuang, on the edge of the desert, before crossing to Baghdad by 794 H2.

The dashed ring is Talas, 751, where captured Chinese papermakers supposedly taught Samarkand. Historian Jonathan Bloom calls the story "unlikely to be factual": paper was already in use in Central Asia H2.

From Cairo and Fez, paper entered Europe through Xàtiva in Spain by 1056. Fabriano added gelatin sizing and the first watermarks in 1282; Nuremberg had a mill in 1390 H3.

Mills reached the Americas with colonists: Mexico City by 1575, Germantown near Philadelphia in 1690. But the first point in that lane is amate, bark paper from western Mexico around 75 BCE, invented independently by beating bark rather than pulping it, so strictly not paper at all.

Seventy thousand to two hundred ninety-five

70,000households · 1901

In 1901 about seventy thousand Japanese households made washi, often as winter work between harvests. By 1984 fewer than five hundred remained, and in 2009 the national association counted 295 H5. Each dot is one household. Scroll and watch them go out.

The world, meanwhile, makes more paper than ever: about 401 million tonnes a year, more than double 1980, with packaging rising while printing papers fall to their lowest since 1987 H5.

III · Surface

Reading a surface

light elevation 80°

Under light from above, a sheet of laid paper looks almost blank. Its relief is there, a few microns high, but flat light hides it.

Conservators lower a single lamp until it skims the surface. In this raking light every ridge throws a shadow, and the sheet shows how it was made R3.

Fine laid lines, 7 to 10 per centimetre, come from the dense wires of the mould. The heavier chain lines, 22 to 32 mm apart, mark the wires sewn to its wooden ribs M3.

Hold the sheet to a window and the reading changes. Paper is thinner over the wires, so they glow. So does the watermark, a wire shape sewn to the mould; Fabriano's are the earliest, from 1282 H3.

Hot, not, rough

Watercolour paper comes in three surfaces. Move acrossTap the swatches to move the light.

Hot-pressed sheets pass between heated plates; cold-pressed (“NOT”, for not hot-pressed) are pressed against textured felts; rough barely at all. Makers publish no roughness figures for these, so the swatches are drawn from descriptions M3.

Ink meets fibre

ClickTap either sheet to drop ink.

Liquid creeps into paper by capillarity, roughly as the Lucas–Washburn law says: the distance grows with the square root of time, fast at first, then slower M5. Sizing raises the angle at which water meets the fibre; near 90° the pull vanishes and the ink stays where the brush left it. Raw xuan has no size at all, which is why a painter's stroke blooms into a soft cloud, its edge softened further by the short fibres of rice straw.

IV · Rendering

Drawing paper on a screen

1 / 7 · gradient noise

1 · Noise

Start with Ken Perlin's gradient noise: a random slope at every grid point, blended smoothly between them. His 2002 revision blends with 6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³ so the surface has no creases R1.

2 · Fractal sum

One layer is too smooth. Add copies at double the frequency and half the strength, octave after octave: fbm(p) = Σ ½ᵏ · noise(2ᵏ p). This is fractal Brownian motion, and it is what SVG's feTurbulence computes R1.

3 · Formation

Low, slow noise becomes the cloudy formation of the sheet, the flocs you see against a window. Real paper is thicker and thinner in patches a few millimetres across.

4 · Fibres

Scatter curved strokes with lengths drawn from a skewed distribution, the way Guo and Kunii modelled rice paper in 1991 as randomly placed fibres R4. Long and few for washi; short and many for copy paper.

5 · Normals

Treat the result as a height field. The slope at each point, from neighbouring heights, gives a surface normal: N = normalize(−∂h/∂x, −∂h/∂y, 1). Shown here in false colour R3.

6 · Light

Light it with Lambert's law, brightness proportional to N·L. Keep the light low, as conservators do, and the tooth appears.

7 · Paper

Tint it, let thin patches glow a little, and tear the edge. The swatches in this essay are made this way, once, when they scroll into view.

Why this page uses masks, not filters

The quickest way to texture text on the web is an SVG filter. But WebKit, the engine behind every iPhone browser, renders filters at screen-pixel resolution and then scales them up, so filtered text turns soft on a retina display; the attribute that once fixed this was removed in 2018 R2.

The ink on these letters is instead a CSS mask: a tile of noise that lets more or less of each letter through. The letters stay sharp at any resolution; only their density varies. On phones the grain is lighter, because at small sizes fine noise reads as blur.

.ink {
  mask-image: url("noise-tile.svg");
  mask-size: 320px 320px;
}

Coda

Paper is the most ordinary surface we touch and one of the most made. Every sheet holds a record of its fibre, its mould and its drying, and a little of the place it came from.
