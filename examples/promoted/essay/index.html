A simulation you can run

Promoted

“In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence.” Laurence J. Peter wrote it as a joke in 1969. Forty years later, two physicists and a sociologist built the company and ran it.

After Pluchino, Rapisarda & Garofalo, “The Peter principle revisited: a computational study”, Physica A 389 (2010) · Ig Nobel Prize in Management, 2010

01 · The rules

A company on six floors

160 people

The company is a pyramid: 81 people on the bottom floor, then 41, 21, 11, 5, and one boss at the top. Everyone starts somewhere between 18 and 40 years old, around 25 on average.

Competence

Each person has a competence from 1 to 10: how well they do the job they hold now. It starts as a bell curve around 7. Here, the darker someone’s trousers, the better they are at their job.

Higher floors matter more

The top job weighs most. Each floor’s work counts with a weight: 1.0 for the boss, then 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2 for the bottom floor. A bad boss costs five times what a bad junior does.

Every year, some leave

Anyone whose competence falls below 4 is fired. Anyone older than 60 retires. Their desks go empty.

Then the ladder moves

Empty desks are filled from the top down: one person from the floor below is promoted into each gap, which opens a gap on their old floor, and so on down. New hires fill the bottom.

Theory one: common sense

Good at your job, good at the next one. Under the common sense theory, a promoted person keeps their competence, give or take a point.

Theory two: Peter’s

But a great salesperson can be a poor sales manager: the new job needs different skills. Under the Peter hypothesis, competence in the new job has nothing to do with the old one. It’s drawn fresh from the same bell curve.

One number for the company

The company’s efficiency adds up everyone’s competence, floor by floor, times that floor’s weight, as a share of a company where everyone scores 10. A brand-new company scores about 70%.

05 · Flip a coin

When you don’t know which world you’re in

Promoting the best wins big under common sense and loses big under Peter’s rule; promoting the worst does the opposite. Real managers don’t know which theory holds. So mix: each time, pick the best with probability p, otherwise the worst.

06 · What it doesn’t prove

A cartoon of a company

The model is deliberately crude. It shows what follows if competence doesn’t carry over between jobs, not that it doesn’t.

Skills do partly carry over

Real promotions sit between the two theories. Reality is probably somewhere along the mixing line in chapter 5, which is exactly why hedging helps.

Nobody learns

Competence never grows with experience or training here, and nobody is moved back down when a promotion fails.

Promotions motivate

A random promotion scheme might wreck morale. The model has no incentives: people don’t work harder to get promoted.

Managers can test

Companies can try people out in the new role before promoting them, which makes the Peter hypothesis less true. The paper’s point is cheaper: if you can’t, don’t assume.

Kicked upstairs

Promoting the best feels like the only fair rule. In this model it is the best rule only when skills carry over from one job to the next. When they don’t, it lifts every good worker out of the job they’re good at and parks them in one they aren’t, floor after floor, until the company is run by people at their level of incompetence. The authors’ advice is uncomfortable but cheap: if you don’t know which world you’re in, a little randomness protects you from the worst of both.
