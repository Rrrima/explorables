**Answer: yes, though it's limited.** The best-known real-world test looked at almost exactly the case in scene 5: salespeople promoted to sales manager. Firms did tend to promote their top sellers, and those top sellers turned out to be weaker managers than the people promoted with lower sales [1]. The study finds a measurable cost on average. It doesn't show that everyone ends up incompetent, and the authors offer more than one explanation [1].

One thing to make clear: the Pluchino–Rapisarda–Garofalo study the video is built on is a computer simulation. It shows what would happen *if* skills don't carry over to the new job. It isn't evidence that this happens in real companies [3]. The real-world evidence comes from the study below.

**Facts for a 30-second answer**

- **The study:** Benson, Li and Shue, "Promotions and the Peter Principle." Working paper February 2018 [1]. Published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* in 2019 [2].
- **Scale:** 53,035 sales workers at 214 US firms in different industries, 2005–2011. 1,531 of them were promoted into management [1].
- **Before the promotion:** if a worker's sales doubled, their chance of promotion rose by 14.3% compared with the usual rate [1].
- **After the promotion:** if a new manager's sales before promotion were double, each person on their team sold about 7.5% less [1]. Put the other way, managers who had been weaker sellers had teams that did better [1].
- **The authors' description:** they call it the first large-scale evidence that firms reward current performance "at the expense of promoting the best potential managers" [1].
- **Not proven to be a mistake (keep this caveat):** the authors offer two explanations. Firms may simply be getting it wrong. Or promising promotions to top sellers may motivate the whole sales force enough to be worth the cost of weaker managers [1]. They lean toward the second: firms put less weight on sales figures when the manager job carries more responsibility [1].
- **Why sales is a good test:** sales staff are already paid for results, so firms shouldn't need promotions as a reward. Finding the effect there anyway makes the result stronger [1].
- **A different explanation (not checked this session):** economist Edward Lazear (2004) argued that people promoted after a great run were partly lucky, so their performance later tends to fall back toward their normal level, with no special "incompetence" needed [4]. This is close to the simulation's "competence redrawn at random" rule.
- **The original:** Peter and Hull's 1969 book was satire, not research [3].

**Animation ideas:** two bars for the same top seller. "Promotion odds +14.3%" goes up, then "team sales −7.5%" goes down. Caption: "53,035 sellers · 214 firms · 2005–2011".

**Script note:** the evidence supports "a great salesperson often makes a *weaker* sales manager." "Terrible" goes further than the data.
