An illustrated essay · with a research report

Sit, Stay, Think

In 1994, 199 obedience judges ranked the dog breeds, from the Border Collie down to the Afghan HoundD1. In 2022, a study of 18,385 dogs found that breed explains about 9% of how any one dog behavesD7.

So how do you measure the mind of a dog, or a cat? This essay looks at the tests, the ranking, the emotions, and the animal that won't sit any of them.

01 · History

A horse that could count

Before anyone ranked breeds, people read animal minds from posture. Darwin drew the same dog twice: stiff and bristling, then low and waggingH9.

In 1898 Edward Thorndike shut cats in puzzle boxes and timed their escapes. One cat took 160 seconds the first time and about 6 by its 24th tryH1. The curve fell slowly. There was no sudden moment of insight.

In 1904 Berlin was taken with Clever Hans, a horse who tapped out sums. With blinders on, he was right 89% of the time when he could see his questioner and 6% when he couldn'tH6. Hans wasn't doing arithmetic. He was reading tiny movements of the questioner's head.

In 1912 William Stern coined the IQ: mental age divided by age, times 100H22. It only works when everyone sits the same test. That's hard enough for children, and harder still for a beagle and a cat.

So the most famous dog ranking isn't a test at all. In 1994 the psychologist Stanley Coren mailed questionnaires to obedience judges and asked them which breeds learn fastestD1.

Then came the genes. A study of 14,000 dogs in 2019 and one of 18,385 dogs in 2022 asked how much of a dog's behaviour its breed explainsD6D7. Their answers are the spine of this essay.

02 · The ranking

The league table

199 obedience judges answered Coren. Each named the ten breeds they found easiest to train and the ten hardest. 190 of them put the Border Collie in their top tenD1.

Breeds that at least 100 judges had rated were ranked: about 130 breeds sharing 79 placesD2. These are the top ten: herders, retrievers and guard dogs, all bred to work for people.

At the bottom are hounds, guardians and the toy and ancient breeds. Last, at 79, is the Afghan Hound. 121 judges placed it in their bottom tenD1. Hover a dog for its rank.

What the tiers measure is repetitions. A top-tier dog learns a new command in fewer than 5 and obeys the first time 95% of the time. A bottom-tier dog may need 80 to 100 or moreD2.

Coren called this working and obedience intelligence, one of three kinds, and said so himself: it ranks trainability, not clevernessD1.

03 · The test room

An IQ test, one skill at a time

Labs don't ask judges. They test one ability at a time. In the classic pointing test, food goes under one of two cups and a person points at it. Most dogs follow the point. Hand-raised wolves mostly don'tD25.

The cylinder test measures self-control. A treat sits inside a clear tube that's open at both ends. A dog that lunges at the plastic fails. A dog that walks around to the opening passesD23.

In 1,508 dogs of 36 breeds, breeds differed much more on some skills than on others. Self-control was strongly breed-linked (0.70), reading people less so (0.39), and reasoning and memory hardly at allD11.

So no single score comes out. In a Finnish battery, Border Collies were best at the cylinder and Kelpies and Malinois best at following gestures. No breed beat any other at logic or memoryD23. Larger breeds tend to do better on memory and self-control, and they have larger brainsD10.

04 · Breed and the individual

Nine percent

Breed averages are real. Across 101 breeds, trainability is one of the most heritable differences between breeds (0.73)D6.

But ask about one dog, and breed tells you little. In 18,385 dogs, breed explained about 9 in every 100 parts of the variation in behaviour between individuals. Depending on the trait, it ranged from 3 to 25D7.

Here is what 9% looks like. Two breeds whose averages differ by exactly that much: the clouds overlap almost completely. Plenty of the "slower" breed outscore the "brighter" one. Meet a few.

The clouds are an illustration built to the 9% figure, not measured dogs. Popularity doesn't help either: a breed's popularity tracks its behaviour in none of 56 testsH21.

05 · EQ

Reading faces

There's no EQ test for dogs. The nearest standard tool, the C-BARQ, is an owner questionnaire about temperamentE22. So researchers test the pieces of emotional intelligence instead, starting with faces.

Dogs have a small muscle that lifts the inner eyebrow, the "puppy eyes" look. In the wolves dissected, there were only scant fibresE15. It seems we bred dogs for a face that reads well to us.

They read us back. Played a happy or angry voice, 17 dogs looked longer at the matching face in 67% of trialsE8. When a person sat and cried, 15 of 18 dogs went to them, a stranger as readily as their ownerE9.

They also mind who gets the attention. When owners petted a realistic stuffed dog, about a quarter of 36 dogs snapped at it. Only one did the same for a toy pail or a bookE17.

06 · Cats

The cat that walked away

Cats are tested at home, if they agree to it. In a classic study comparing cats and dogs, 7 of 26 recruited cats dropped out before the testing beganC1. Cat cognition is a much smaller field than dog cognitionC18.

Both species followed pointing equally well. Then each faced a box that couldn't be opened. 12 of 14 dogs turned to look at a person. Only 6 of 14 cats did, and the cats kept working at the box for longerC1. Is that less social intelligence, or more persistence? The test can't tell.

Cats do read us. They know their own namesC2, look to their owner when something strange appears (79%)C10, and attach like infants: 64% of 70 kittens were securely bonded, against about 65% of human babiesC7.

And the breed ranking? There isn't one. The largest study, 5,726 cats of 40 breeds, found differences in temperament but measured no cognitive skill at allC15. Most pet cats aren't pedigreed anywayC22. The "smartest cat breed" lists are owner opinionC26.

07 · Brains

The other EQ

Anatomists have their own EQ, the encephalization quotient: brain size against what's expected for the body. The cat is the yardstick at 1.0, a typical dog is 1.2, and humans are about 7.5E2.

Counting neurons is more telling. One cat's cortex held about 250 million. Two dogs averaged about 528 millionE1. One grain here stands for ten million.

A brown bear's brain is nine times the cat's, yet its cortex held about the same: 251 millionE1. Size isn't the shortcut. And these counts come from one cat and two dogs.

Coda

Which is smarter?

There's no single number. Ask which skill and which animal. A Border Collie learns commands fast, and a Basset Hound follows a scent it was bred for. A cat won't come to the testing table and still knows its name. Breed shifts the averages a little. The dog in front of you is mostly itself.
