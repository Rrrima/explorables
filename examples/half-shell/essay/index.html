The Half Shell

An oyster is a small jar of the water it grew in. To match the sea's saltiness it packs its cells with amino acids, and about 80% of them are one, taurineT2. That is most of what you taste.

This essay takes one platter apart: why people love oysters, why they cost a dollar at five o'clock, why two from different bays taste nothing alike, what the most expensive ones cost, and how to tell a good one. HoverTap an oyster to see where it grew.

1 · Culture

Poverty and oysters

Oysters were not always a treat. For most of history they were the food of whoever lived near the water, and in the 1800s the food of the poor.

The oldest leftovers

On St. Catherines Island, Georgia, a ring of shells 70 m across is about 4,300 years old. Parasitic snails on the shells show people only gathered oysters in the cool monthsC15.

The first oyster farmer

Around 95 BCE, Sergius Orata hung twigs round mature oysters in Lucrine Lake, on the bay of Naples, so the young would settle on them and could be moved to beds he builtC18.

Porter and oysters

In 1830s London they were so cheap that Dickens has Sam Weller remark that "poverty and oysters" seem to go togetherC12. Porters washed them down with dark beer, the porterC25.

All you can eat for six cents

New York's harbour held some 350 square miles of beds when the Dutch arrivedC4. Oyster cellars marked by red globes sold the "Canal Street Plan": all you could eat for 6¢C5. By 1880 the city's waters gave about 700 million oysters a yearC6.

Closed

Sewage and typhoid followed. Bed after bed was condemned, and the last, in Raritan Bay, closed in 1927C4.

A luxury, now

Today an oyster on a US menu averages about $3.34 if it is from the East Coast and $3.71 from the WestQ17. The scarcity, the ritual and the shucker's skill are part of the price.

2 · Happy hour

Why a dollar at five o'clock

The buck-a-shuck deal is newer than it feels, and it is not meant to make money on oysters.

Four fifty-nine

At a New York fish bar in 2019, an oyster cost $3 to $4C1.

Five o'clock

At happy hour the same oyster drops to $1 to $1.50. That bar shucked about 20,000 a week, 6,000 of them at happy hourC1.

Where the dollar goes

The bar pays about 77 to 80¢ for each oysterC1; in Boston, 65¢ to $1.15. The farmer gets about 57¢C2. Add the shucker's wage and the dollar oyster loses money.

The drink pays

It is a loss leader: the cheap oyster fills the seats, and the martinis, entrées and tips pay for itC1C2. Oysters suit it: they set "the tone for a slow, fun meal where you sit and chat and drink"C2.

An old trick

Nineteenth-century saloons gave a salty "free lunch" to anyone who bought a drink, likely first at La Bourse de Maspero, New Orleans, in 1838; a Chicago saloon offered one free oyster per drinkC23. The oyster deal itself came back in the late 1980s at Shaw's Crab House in ChicagoC21, and the words "happy hour" come from US Navy ship socials of 1913C3.

3 · Why it tastes good

A jar of the sea

The taste people chase, briny then sweet then a long savoury finish, comes from how an oyster survives in salt water.

It doesn't fight the salt

Oysters are osmoconformers: instead of pumping salt out, they match the water around them by filling their cells with free amino acidsT1.

Fresh water: bland

In brackish water near 8 parts per thousand, close to the lowest a Pacific oyster toleratesT2, the cells hold few amino acids and the meat tastes washed outT1.

Ocean water: savoury

At full ocean strength, about 35 pptT24, the cells are packed. Taurine is about 80% of themT2, with glycine, alanine and glutamate, the umami oneT1. Drag the salinity yourself.

Salt, sweet, finish

Tasters describe three phases: salt first, then body and sweetness when you chew, from stored glycogen, then a finish that can be floral, fruity or metallicT1T18. Their aphrodisiac fame is folklore from the oyster cellars of 19th-century New York nightlifeC22.

4 · Place

Merroir

Wine has terroir; oysters have merroir. Same species, different water, different taste. A trained panel in Australia found all 12 of its taste attributes differed by growing regionT26.

The briny north

Cold, ocean-fed bays from Prince Edward Island to Long Island give the saltiest East Coast oysters: Wellfleet, "very salty"; Island Creek, "butter and brine"; Moonstone, "stone and iron"T12T11T8.

The mild south

The same species in the brackish Chesapeake tastes of "sweet butter"T8. In the warm Gulf, at 5–15 ppt, oysters never go dormant and stay "mild and soft"T15.

Cucumber and melon

On the Pacific coast, Pacific and Kumamoto oysters bring vegetal notes: Hama Hama, "cucumber, salt and citrus"; Kumamoto, "melon-scented"T14T8. Kusshis are tumbled in trays until the cup is deep and the taste "ultra-clean"T10.

Copper, green, iodine

Europe's native flat oyster has a "brassy… metallic" finishT8. At Marennes-Oléron, oysters finish in clay ponds where a diatom turns their gills green; they taste "salty, then sweet"T1T20. Sydney rock oysters are "rich, iodiney"T27. HoverTap a place.

5 · Season

Months with an R

"Don't eat oysters in months without an R" is printed in an English book of 1599C15. It has a real basis, and it is mostly out of date.

Fat for winter

Through autumn and spring the oyster stores glycogen from plankton blooms. That is the sweetness, and it peaks just before spawningT1.

Spawning

When the water warms past about 20°C, Pacific oysters spawnT6. Summer oysters turn milky and soft, "like heavy cream", then thin and blandQ27Q28.

Warm water, Vibrio

Vibrio bacteria peak from May to OctoberQ22. The CDC estimates 80,000 US vibriosis cases a year, 52,000 from food, most from raw shellfishQ22. A contaminated oyster looks and smells normal.

The rule today

Fast chilling and sterile triploid oysters, which barely spawn, loosened the rule; half of West Coast farmed Pacifics were triploid in 2016Q27. Julie Qiu calls the R rule "a myth you don't have to follow"T18. Wild oysters are still best in the cold months.

6 · The most expensive

Time, rarity and size

The priciest oysters are not a different animal. They are older, rarer, bigger, or handled more. Here they are at true relative size.

A supermarket oyster

Six Loch Fyne rock oysters cost £7.90 at Waitrose, about $1.71 eachQ19. A bistro Pacific is ready in about 18 monthsQ8.

Small and slow

Kumamotos stay tiny, 1 to 2 inches, and take 3 to 5 years: $95 for 30 from the grower, about $3.17 eachQ13.

The Paris pair

At a Paris brasserie, six Gillardeau N°2 cost €36 and six wild flat Belons €39, about $7 eachQ1. Gillardeau grows for up to four years and lasers a "G" on every shell against fakesQ2Q4. Flats are scarce because two parasites cut European yields by about 90% from 1968Q10.

The king

Coffin Bay "King" oysters from South Australia are left for 6 to 7 years. One weighs about 1 kg and is 18 cm long, and sold for A$100, about 60 ordinary oystersQ6Q8. That price is from 2008–2012; no newer one could be confirmedQ9.

7 · Choosing

How to tell a good one

Five checks from food-safety rules and from people who run oyster bars. Click an oyster on the tray to tap it.Tap an oyster on the tray to test it.

Shut tight

A live oyster is closed, or closes when you tap it. If it stays open, or the shell is cracked, throw it outQ20.

Heavy and full

A good oyster is heavy for its size and sits in its own liquor: "fresh and bright and full". Dry and shrunken means it sat too longQ25. Don't pour the liquor offT18.

Smells like the sea

It should smell like the sea, never fishyQ24Q26. Shucked in front of you is best; bad shucking is a reason to send one backQ26.

The tag

Every sack carries a harvest tag. Restaurants must keep it 90 days after the sack is emptyQ21, so a bar can tell you where and when your oyster came out of the water.

Cold and quick

Keep them at 35–45°F, cup side down under a damp towel, never in waterQ24, and eat them within about a weekQ25. French advice says 4–5 daysQ30.

Coda

The empty shells

An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a dayC11. In the 1800s the Chesapeake's oysters could filter the whole bay in 3 or 4 days; today it takes nearly a year, because fewer than 1% are leftC9. Worldwide, about 85% of oyster reefs are goneC26.

So the shells are worth returning. New York's Billion Oyster Project has collected 3.2 million pounds of them to rebuild reefs in the harbour that once held half the world's oysters, by one estimateC8C6. Most oysters you eat today are farmed, and farmed oysters filter the water they grow in too.
