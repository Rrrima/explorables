Research report

The Half Shell — research report

The evidence behind The Half Shell, an illustrated essay about oysters. Compiled 18 October 2026.

About this report

This report asks five questions. Why do people love oysters? Why are oysters the classic happy-hour food? How does the place an oyster grows change its taste? Which oysters are the most expensive, and why? And how do you tell a good one from a bad one?

The research was done on 18 October 2026 in three parallel web research tracks: culture and history (sources tagged C), taste, biology and place (T), and price and quality (Q). Pages were fetched and read, not taken from search summaries. Where a page could not be read and only a search snippet was seen, the claim is marked as snippet-only. Every number was recorded with its unit, year and range.

When sources disagree, both values are kept and the conflict is shown rather than smoothed over. Values that are weak, secondhand or unverified carry a small (flagged) marker, and all of them are collected under Open questions. A tag such as C1 links to the full source.

Prices in other currencies are converted to US dollars at approximate October 2026 rates: EUR 1 = USD 1.10, GBP 1 = USD 1.30, AUD 1 = USD 0.65 and AED 1 = USD 0.272. The dollar figures are rough guides, not exact conversions.

Chapter C

Culture & history

C1Middens and deep history

People have eaten oysters for thousands of years, and the shells they threw away are still piled up. These heaps are called middens.

On the Damariscotta River in Maine, people used the river as early as about 5,000 years ago. Oysters became established there about 2,400 years ago, and the middens built up over about 1,000 years, roughly 200 BC to AD 1000 C14.

The Whaleback midden was originally more than 30 ft (9 m) deep and more than 1,650 ft (500 m) long. Most of it was dug up for chicken feed between 1886 and 1891 by the Damariscotta Shell and Fertilizer Co. The site now covers 11 acres C14. Other, unverified figures for its size circulate (flagged).

In New York, the oldest oyster midden, at Dobbs Ferry on the Hudson, is dated to about 6950 BC C5 (flagged). That date comes secondhand through a book review.

On St. Catherines Island, Georgia, a shell ring about 4,300 years old and 230 ft (70 m) wide holds a clue about when people ate. Tiny parasitic snails on the shells show that the harvest was limited to late fall, winter and spring. That makes it the oldest evidence of something like the "R-month" rule C15.

C2Romans and Sergius Orata

The first known oyster farmer is the Roman Gaius Sergius Orata, who flourished around 95 BC at Lucrine Lake near Baiae in Campania. He placed twigs around mature oysters so that the young (spat) would attach to them and could be moved. He also built artificial beds enclosed by channels and dams C18.

His fame was such that Crassus joked that "even if Orata were deprived of the waters of the lake, he would still find oysters on his roof-tiles" C18.

C3Poverty food: Dickens and the London porters

In 19th-century London, oysters were food for the poor. In The Pickwick Papers (1836–37, ch. 22), Sam Weller remarks: "It's a wery remarkable circumstance, Sir… that poverty and oysters seems to go together" C12. A more polished version, "always seem to go together", is often quoted; the wording should be checked against the printed text (flagged).

Porters in London's theatre district drank dark beer and ate oysters "because they were very cheap" C25.

C4New York harbor and the Canal Street Plan

When the Dutch arrived, the lower Hudson estuary held about 350 square miles (about 900 km²) of oyster beds C4. A separate figure of about 220,000 acres of historic reef C7 works out to about 344 square miles, so the two agree. By one biologist's estimate, passed on by Mark Kurlansky, the harbor held half the world's oysters C6 C4 (flagged). That is an estimate, not a measurement.

In 1609 the Lenape offered oysters to Henry Hudson C6. The first oyster cellar opened in a Broad Street basement in 1763 C5; one source says 1783 C21 (flagged).

The "Canal Street Plan" offered all the oysters you could eat for 6 cents C5 C13 C22. Oyster bars on Canal Street were opened by free African Americans after New York ended slavery in 1827 C5. The cellars were marked by red globes, and oysters were "a food of the night" C22. No exact years for the Plan were found (flagged).

The trade was enormous. In 1842 New York consumed about $6 million worth of oysters. In the 1850s nearly 1,000 men worked on oyster farms. By 1860 the business sold more than 12 million oysters a year, and by 1880 New York waters produced about 700 million oysters a year with "scientific cultivation" C5 C6. All of these come secondhand from Kurlansky (flagged).

Oysters were also spectacle. In 1885 the shucker Billy Lowney opened 100 oysters in 3 minutes 3 seconds, and Diamond Jim Brady reportedly began meals with six dozen Lynnhaven oysters C6.

C5Collapse

Sewage ended it. Starting in the 1880s, New York's beds were closed one by one as disease outbreaks were traced to them. The last bed, in Raritan Bay, closed in 1927 C4.

Sources disagree on the details. One says a 1916 typhoid epidemic led to a ban on oystering C5; another says the beds were closed "by 1930" C6; an unread snippet gives 1923 (flagged). The safest summary is that the beds closed in the 1920s. A typhoid scare in the 1920s and Prohibition, which closed the taverns that were "the oyster's primary habitat", also cut demand C21.

C6Happy hour: where the term comes from

"Happy hour" did not start with drinking. Social clubs, mostly women's, used names like "Happy Hour Club" from at least the early 1880s C3.

On the battleship USS Arkansas, a group called the "Happy Hour Social" organized twice-weekly smokers in early 1913. By June 1913 the crew called them "happy hours". They featured boxing, wrestling, music, dancing and films, not alcohol. By the end of the First World War the custom had spread through the Navy C3.

Speakeasies during Prohibition are said to have held "cocktail hours" before dinner, but this claim lacks a citation (flagged). The drinking sense appears in print in the Saturday Evening Post of 25 April 1959, with earlier uses from 1951 near California naval bases C3.

C7The free lunch

The ancestor of happy hour is the 19th-century saloon "free lunch". Anyone who bought a drink could eat, and the drink sales paid for the food C24. The food was mostly salty, to make people thirsty C23.

Oysters were part of it from the start. In Chicago, "Chesterfield Joe" Mackin is said to have offered one free oyster per drink. The likelier origin, according to the historian Christine Sismondo, is La Bourse de Maspero in New Orleans in 1838. An 1875 New York Times piece describes a New Orleans free lunch with oyster soup C23.

A survey of 634 Minneapolis saloons in 1899 rated 3 free lunches "elaborate", 8 "excellent", 50 "good", 88 "fair" and 77 "poor"; about 408 offered none. In Chicago in 1901, "nearly all" of 115 saloons offered one. The custom was largely gone by 1917 C24.

Whether salt really sells drinks is unproven. A small 2014 study (58 people) found similar thirst after salted, sweet or plain nuts (flagged); it was seen only as a snippet. Treat "salt makes you drink" as bar lore.

C8The cheap-oyster happy hour, since the late 1980s

In the late 1980s, Shaw's Crab House in Chicago offered half-price oysters. It was "one of the first places to introduce a designated oyster deal", and the idea spread. Oyster happy hours helped the oyster's comeback in that decade, together with cleaner water after the Clean Water Act C21.

In 1996, Old Ebbitt Grill in Washington, DC (founded 1856) started a late-night half-price oyster hour, from 11 pm to 1 am at four bars. It sold about 985,000 oysters in 2010 C22.

Supply caught up. North American oyster farming "exploded between 2010 and 2015" thanks to disease-resistant oysters, plastic mesh bags and floating gear, and there are about 300 farms in the Northeast C20. It takes 18 months to 4 years to grow an oyster from 2 mm to market size C1.

Why oysters? They "set the tone for a slow, fun meal where you sit and chat and drink" C2. A chef is quoted saying they "keep well, turn out consistently, and require little labor" (flagged), but only in a snippet. The critic Julie Qiu says the deals made oysters accessible but taught people to expect them to be cheap and all alike C1.

C10Drink pairings

The Dutch in New Amsterdam ate oysters with beer in taverns. Modern bars pour Champagne, sherry (manzanilla and fino), gin and tonic, and whiskey C21.

Stout has the longest story. Porter beer came from 18th-century London, where porters ate cheap oysters with it. In 1837 Disraeli wrote of dining at the Carlton on "oysters, Guinness and broiled bones" C25. The popular pairing with Muscadet and Chablis was not verified in any fetched source (flagged).

C11Why people love them, and the status flip

Part of the appeal is reputation. Kurlansky ties 19th-century New York oysters to nightlife, vice and their name as an aphrodisiac C22. The story of Casanova eating 50 oysters for breakfast appears only in popular retellings and should be treated as legend (flagged). Oysters are rich in zinc, but the figures found were snippets (flagged), and there is no evidence that zinc makes them an aphrodisiac.

Part is ritual: shucking contests C6 and a late-night oyster culture among restaurant workers C22.

The oldest piece of folk wisdom is the "R-month" rule. It appears in the 1599 English book Dyets Dry Dinner: "It is unseasonable and unwholesome in all months that have not an R in their names to eat an oyster" C15. The author is given variously as Henry Buttes, William Butler or Samuel Butler C17 (flagged). The real reasons behind it are summer spawning (May to October in the Southeast) C15, watery summer meat and Vibrio bacteria C16.

Then there is price. Oysters were dirt cheap in 1830s London C12 C25 and all-you-can-eat for 6¢ in New York C5. In New Orleans around 1900 a dozen cost 10–15¢ C17. Today a single northern oyster runs about $3.50 C17, and New York raw bars charge $3–4 each C1. The poor man's food became a luxury, and happy hour has partly turned it back C1.

C12Production

Most oysters today are farmed. World oyster aquaculture was more than 7.5 million tonnes in 2023, up 50% from 2014. China produces about 89%, Korea about 4%, and the US and Japan about 2% each. The wild catch is only about 101,676 tonnes, about 60% of it from the US C19.

US oyster output in 2022, wild and farmed together, was 29.7 million lb worth $199 million C11. A figure of 44.7 million lb of meat for the 2018 aquaculture peak uses a different measure and was seen only as a snippet (flagged). No figure for US consumption per person was found.

C13Filtration and reefs

An oyster feeds by pumping water through its gills, and in doing so it cleans the water. An adult filters up to 50 US gallons (about 190 L) a day C11, or "more than 50 gallons… under certain conditions" C9. Treat 50 gallons as an upper bound under ideal conditions.

The Chesapeake Bay shows what was lost. In the late 19th century its oysters could filter the whole Bay in 3–4 days; today it takes nearly a year. The population is under 1% of historic levels C9 C10. Harvests were more than 1.5 million bushels a year in the 1850s and about 20 million bushels a year by the 1880s C9. Maryland's 1898 harvest was 15 million bushels, about 5.3 billion oysters C10. Other sites put 15 million bushels in the 1850s, a tenfold disagreement (flagged).

Two diseases followed: Dermo, found in 1949, and MSX, around 1959. By 2024, reefs had been restored in 9 of 10 targeted tributaries C9.

Worldwide, about 85% of oyster reefs have been lost, according to a 2011 study of 144 bays and 44 ecoregions. In many bays more than 90% are gone, and some are "functionally extinct", with under 1% left C26.

C14The Billion Oyster Project

Founded in 2014, New York's Billion Oyster Project aims to return one billion oysters to the harbor. At that number, the oysters would filter the harbor about every 3 days C1 C7.

By February 2024 it had placed 122 million oysters, with 84 million more planned that year C7. It has collected 3.2 million lb of shell and engaged 154,000 people C8; in 2019 it was collecting about 8,000 lb of shell a week C1. The target year is 2030 on the project's own homepage C8 but 2035 in a 2024 news report C7 (flagged).

Chapter T

Taste, biology & place

T1Osmoconformers: amino acids and taurine

The key to an oyster's taste is how it copes with salt. Oysters are osmoconformers: instead of pumping salt out, they match the salt strength of the surrounding water by filling their cells with free amino acids. In Pacific oysters the most abundant are glycine, alanine, serine, aspartic acid and glutamic acid, plus taurine T1.

Taurine is the largest share, about 80% of total amino acid content in the Pacific oyster (C. gigas). Across marine invertebrates it makes up 60–80% of free amino acids T2. The oyster does not regulate sodium, calcium or magnesium, and the salt strength of its blood can fall from 800 to 380 mOsm/kg within 8 hours of a change in the water T2.

Pacific oysters can face swings from below 10 to above 35 parts per thousand (ppt) of salt. The lowest known tolerated level is about 8 ppt, and 5 ppt may be too low T2. The FAO gives an optimum of 20–25 ppt, survival above 35 ppt, and a temperature range of −1.8 to 35 °C T6.

T2Salinity sets flavour

Because the amino acids track the salt, the water shapes the taste. In low-salinity water oysters lose amino acids and taste bland and "washed out". In high-salinity water they gain them and a "rich umami taste comes through" T1. This is why salty sites are said to have good "merroir", the sea's version of terroir.

The source is a review essay rather than a tasting trial, so this is best read as an established physiological mechanism with a plausible effect on taste. Note also that saltier water means more savoury richness, not necessarily more sweetness. Sweetness comes mostly from glycogen and season T1 T3 T5.

Diet matters too. Diatoms such as Skeletonema costatum and Navicula are "known to impart good flavor", and sites with frequent blooms of these plankton have the best merroir T1.

T3Season and glycogen

Glycogen is the starch-like sugar store that makes an oyster sweet and plump. In spring, plankton blooms drive gonad ripening and glycogen storage, so oysters are fattest and sweetest just before their late-spring or early-summer spawn. After spawning the meat is thin and the glycogen is spent, so they taste bland. They rebuild glycogen during the autumn blooms before winter dormancy T1. Popular lore favours winter oysters; both views fit this cycle.

Triploid oysters, which cannot spawn, show the contrast. In Korean triploids measured in 2015, glycogen peaked in summer at 1.26 ± 0.03 g/100 g and was 0.69–0.86 g/100 g in other seasons. Total amino acids were highest in winter (1,011.5 mg/100 g) and lowest in summer (486.7 mg/100 g), led by taurine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, glycine and alanine T5. A summer triploid keeps its sugar, which partly undoes the R-month rule.

Japan's summer oyster, the Iwagaki (C. nippona), loses glycogen from May to September and spawns in August–September. The Pacific oyster's glycogen is already low in July, and it spawns July–September T4.

T4Spawning and growth

Pacific oysters generally spawn when water is above 20 °C, rarely at 15–18 °C. A female 8–15 cm long releases 50–200 million eggs per spawning T6. An Eastern oyster female can produce about 100 million eggs a year T23, or more than 100 million in one event T25. Eastern oysters spawn in early summer as temperature and salinity rise T23.

Most oysters under a year old are male and most older ones are female T23. The top shell is flat and the bottom one cupped, with a purple muscle scar inside. Eastern oysters are 3–5 in long, up to 8 in, and live up to 20 years T23 T25.

Growth times vary widely. Pacific oysters reach 70–100 g (over 75 mm) in about 18–30 months T6. Sydney rock oysters reach about 60 g in about 3 years T27 T28. Kumamotos are slow, at 25–27 g after about 1.5 years T22. Malpeques and Maine Belons grow wild for 3–4 years T29 T30.

T5Flavour chemistry

When researchers rebuilt an oyster extract from pure chemicals, the main taste compounds were free amino acids and mineral ions (sodium, potassium, chloride, phosphate). Nucleotides, mainly AMP, and quaternary ammonium bases played a supporting role, and succinic acid was the main organic acid. The synthetic mix reproduced the taste well T31.

A sensory study of 31 Japanese brands found that more glycogen predicted "sweet" or "rich" ratings. Total amino acids went with "sweet" and serine with "rich". Taurine was the most abundant, then glutamic acid, glycine, alanine and proline T3 (flagged). Only the abstract was read.

Place changes the chemistry. Oysters from 8 regions of Guangdong, China, differed in glutamic acid, alanine, glycine, threonine, arginine and succinic acid. During fattening, glycine, glutamic acid, alanine, proline and IMP changed most, and taste quality peaked after 3 months T32.

T6Merroir, measured by a trained panel

The best evidence that merroir is real comes from an Australian CSIRO pilot study. Ten trained tasters scored Pacific oysters from New South Wales, Tasmania and South Australia. All 12 sensory attributes differed significantly by growing region, and the batches also differed in moisture, glycogen and taste-active amino acids T26.

The peer-reviewed version built a 12-term vocabulary that detected differences across the 3 growing areas (seen as an abstract only). The authors caution that the samples were not a robust regional comparison T26.

T8Cucumber, melon and metal

Pacific oysters are famous for green, fruity notes. Julie Qiu describes a "distinct vegetal and cucumber note" and a creamier texture in West Coast oysters T18. Rowan Jacobsen calls Kumamotos "famously melon-scented", finds "hints of muskmelon" in Nootka Sound oysters, and likens Penn Cove to "a cucumber sandwich rolled in parsley" T8. Hama Hama oysters taste of cucumber, salt and citrus T14.

The chemistry is not settled. Snippets point to (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal, the classic cucumber and melon compound, as a key odorant in oyster extract T-S4, and to 2-nonanol as the source of a cucumber note in raw Pacific oysters T-S3 (flagged). That these form from omega-3 fats is a plausible inference, not a shown result.

European flat oysters (Ostrea edulis) taste of metal instead. Rice notes "a slight metallic finish" in flats and Olympias T1. Jacobsen calls the flat "brassy… because it tastes like metal" and speaks of a "refined, metallic zing" T8 T9. A Maine grower describes an "intense, lingering copper finish" T30, and UK tasting notes use zinc, copper, tin and bronze T19.

The obvious explanation, that flats contain more copper, is not supported. No fetched source links measured copper or zinc to the taste, and one snippet found Pacific oysters generally higher in both (flagged).

T10Places and growing methods

Marennes-Oléron, France: green oysters. Oysters are finished in claires, shallow clay ponds that were once salt marshes. The diatom Haslea ostrearia releases a blue-green pigment, marennine, that stains the gills green. Growers manage the ponds for Skeletonema to fatten the oysters and Haslea to green them. Louis XIV was reportedly fond of the green ones T1.

The rules are precise. A Fine de Claire Verte (Label Rouge) spends at least 28 days in the claires, between November and March, at no more than 3 kg/m². It tastes "salty, then sweet" and is sold October to May to avoid milky, spawning oysters T20. A Pousse en Claire is grown at no more than 5 oysters per m² for 4–8 months, and tastes sweet, fleshy, vegetal and of iodine T21. Green oysters fetch about twice the price; Jacobsen tasted "truffle-like… spinach soufflé" T16. Snippets say finishing in the claires has been suspended June to August since 2024 because summer heat makes them too salty (flagged).

Kusshi, Deep Bay, British Columbia: tumbling. A Pacific oyster grown in floating trays and tumbled hard. Tumbling breaks the thin growing edge, so the shell deepens and thickens instead of spreading. It reaches about 2 in, nearly as deep as it is long, with an "ultra-clean flavor" T10.

Island Creek, Duxbury, Massachusetts: tide. The shallow bay exchanges nearly all its water twice a day: 35 billion gallons at high tide against 7 billion at low, with 15–20 million oysters in it at any time. The taste is "butter-and-brine", "as salty as all getout" T11.

T11How to taste, in three phases

Tasting an oyster happens in three phases T1:

  1. Salt. The liquor and the first contact are briny.
  2. Body and sweetness. These come when you chew. As Julie Qiu puts it, "That's when you chew into an oyster — you will actually taste sweetness in the body" T18.
  3. Finish. Floral, fruity or metallic. West Coast finishes linger; others stop abruptly T18.

The official descriptor for a Fine de Claire Verte, "salty, then sweet", follows the same order T20. Qiu's advice: eat the first oyster "naked", don't pour off the liquor, and expect minerality to taste "like licking a rock". She calls the R-month rule "a myth you don't have to follow" today T18. British tasters score nose, body, finish and texture, with 1–10 scores for flavour, saltiness, sweetness and umami T19.

Chapter Q

Price & quality

Q1Gillardeau

Gillardeau is the best-known luxury oyster brand. Henri Gillardeau founded the house in 1898 at Bourcefranc-le-Chapus, in Marennes-Oléron Q3 Q5. Since October 2014 every oyster has been laser-engraved with a "G", explicitly against counterfeits Q3 Q4.

The house calls itself the "Rolls-Royce of oysters". Each oyster is inspected for shape and meat content, cleaned of parasitic shellfish and sorted by size by hand Q4. A retailer says they grow for "up to four years" Q2; the house gives no figure, and a snippet says 3–5 years (flagged). An ordinary French oyster reaches market size in about 3 years Q29, so the premium buys a longer, more hands-on grow-out, not a different animal.

About half of Gillardeau oysters are produced in Normandy and half in County Cork, Ireland Q5 (flagged), based on a 2008 report that may be dated. A snippet reports fake "G"s engraved onto cheaper oysters in China (flagged).

At Le Dôme in Paris (2026), a Spéciale Gillardeau N°2 costs €36 for six (€6.00 each) and a N°5 €24 for six. An ordinary Fine de Claire N°3 costs the same €24, and a N°2 €28.50 Q1. In Dubai, Gillardeau N°2 retails at AED 320 per dozen, about $7.25 each Q2.

Q2Belon and the flat oysters

The European flat oyster (Ostrea edulis) is scarce because of disease. Two parasites, Marteilia refringens in 1968–70 and Bonamia ostreae about 11 years later, cut flat-oyster yields tenfold, about 90%. European production was about 15,000–20,000 tonnes in 1968–70; today it is about 2,000 tonnes a year in France and about 6,000 in Europe Q10. Other papers give a higher peak (flagged), but all agree on a roughly 90% decline. Flats are now about 1–2% of French oyster output Q31.

The price follows. At Le Dôme, flat oysters (plates TG, Maison Cadoret) cost €39 for six, €6.50 each, the most expensive oyster on the menu Q1. Wild Maine "Belons" sell for $36 a dozen in winter only Q11, or $119 for 50, each banded shut by hand Q12.

Q3The Coffin Bay King

The single most expensive oyster in the sources is the Coffin Bay "King" from South Australia, at A$100 each (about $65). That price is reported at a 2008 auction Q6, by ABC in 2011 Q7 and at the Port Lincoln Hotel in 2012 Q8. A King weighs about 1 kg with its shell, is about 18 cm long and is 6–7 years old, against about 1.5 years for a bistro oyster Q8. It has "up to 10 times extra meat" Q6.

In 2008 normal South Australian oysters cost about A$20 a dozen in restaurants, so one King cost about as much as 60 ordinary oysters Q6. Claims of "several hundred dollars" are unsourced, and no source after 2012 confirms the King is still sold (flagged). One writer calls it "theater… not representative of everyday pricing" Q9.

Q4Kumamoto and Kusshi

Small premium oysters cost more because they take longer. Taylor Shellfish sells Kumamotos at $95 for 30 ($3.17 each). They are 1–2 in across and take 3–5 years to reach market size, from seed traced to 1940s Japanese imports. On the same farm, Shigoku cost $80 and Pacific Petites $65 for 30 Q13.

Kusshis from Stellar Bay, BC, are farmed for 3–4 years, "tumbled repeatedly to form a smooth, golf-ball shaped oyster", average 2.25 in and are graded "to the millimeter" Q14. No retail price was fetched; restaurant prices were seen only in snippets (flagged).

Q6Typical prices and the markup

In 2021 US restaurant menus, an oyster cost on average $3.71 if grown on the West Coast, $3.34 from the East Coast and $2.69 from the Gulf ($3.41 when unspecified). By restaurant region: Pacific $3.61, Midwest $3.47, Northeast $3.34, South $2.92 Q17.

Adjusted for inflation, the average restaurant price has stayed in a $2–4 band since 2011, and the "buck-a-shuck" happy-hour price has lost real value Q18. A snippet gives $3.32 in 2016 and $3.33 in 2024, flat in real terms (flagged).

Growers get far less. Alaska farm-gate prices were $0.80–1.16 per oyster in 2025, "similar to 2024", with demand down because of "reduced dining out, especially at higher end restaurants" Q15. Nationally, half-shell oysters average $0.40–0.59 at the farm, and in some states "nearly half" of farmed oysters go to lower-value markets at under $0.25. Break-even ranges from $2.57 per oyster on a half-acre farm with 40,000 oysters to $0.52 on 4 acres with 960,000 Q16.

Roughly, then, a $0.50 farm oyster becomes a $3.30 restaurant oyster, a markup of about 6–7 times. In a UK supermarket, six Loch Fyne rock oysters cost £7.90, about £1.32 each Q19. Most US consumers prefer oysters 2.5–3.5 in long with a deep cup Q18.

Q7How to tell a good one

In the shell. A live oyster is closed, or closes when you tap it. "If they don't close when tapped, do not select them." Throw away cracked or broken shells Q20.

The tag. Live shellfish come in tagged sacks or containers Q20. Restaurants must keep the tags for 90 days from the day the container is emptied, in order, without mixing containers Q21. Some states count the 90 days from harvest (flagged). A tag lets you ask where and when an oyster was harvested.

Heavy, wet, sea-smelling. A good oyster is "fresh and bright and full", heavy with liquid; dry and shrunken means it has sat too long Q25. It should be "closed tight" and hold its liquor when shucked; throw it away if it has popped open, is dry inside or smells "fishy". It "should smell like the sea" Q24. Once shucked, the meat "should look really hydrated", with plenty of liquor Q26. In France, a fresh oyster should flinch a little when lemon is dropped on it Q30.

Freshness window. Good oyster bars rarely hold oysters for more than about a week Q25. A grower suggests about a week, longer in winter when oysters are dormant Q24; the French consumer agency says 4–5 days Q30. The range runs from about 4 days to more than 2 weeks; about a week is the safe rule.

The bar. Julie Qiu prefers places that shuck in front of you, have high turnover and knowledgeable staff, and she sends back badly shucked oysters Q26. Chef Michael Serpa avoids raw bars that pile oysters so high that the top ones don't touch the ice Q25.

Q8Storage, and a US–France conflict

US advice is cold. A grower recommends 35–45 °F, cup side down "to ease strain on the muscle", under a damp towel, and never in water or melted ice, because "they will try to feed and die" Q24. The FDA says seafood used within 2 days should be kept at or below 40 °F Q20. Serpa keeps raw shellfish around 35 °F, not frozen Q25.

French advice is warmer. The national consumer agency says to store oysters at 5–15 °C (41–59 °F), out of the light, flat and not turned over so they keep their liquid, for 4–5 days Q30 (flagged). French regulators accept cellar-cool storage; the two countries simply differ.

"Fine" and "Spéciale" describe meatiness, not quality. A Fine de Claire has a fill index of 6.5–10.5 Q30; a Spéciale has more than 10.5 (flagged). The index is drained meat weight divided by total weight, times 100. Scientists use a similar condition index, which drops sharply after spawning; it is the lab version of "heavy for its size".

Q10Season, the R rule, and triploids

In summer, ordinary (diploid) oysters "get flabby as their gonads swell with sperm and eggs", coating "your mouth like heavy cream does" Q27. After spawning, "shell volume plummets, creating a less than desirable eating experience" Q28.

The R rule rests on that texture and on warm-water Vibrio bacteria. A wholesaler argues that rapid chilling and the modern cold chain make the bacterial reason obsolete Q28 (flagged). But that is an industry view, and CDC data show Vibrio still peaks from May to October Q22.

Triploids, developed in the late 1980s, have three sets of chromosomes. They are plumper, grow faster and are less "spawny". In 2016, 50% of US West Coast farmed Pacific oysters were triploid, but the share is falling: Taylor went from about 70% to about 30%, and Hama Hama from 24% triploids in 2022 to 18% now. In lab heat-stress tests, triploids died at 2.5 times the rate of diploids Q27.

Q11Safety and Vibrio

The main risk of raw oysters is Vibrio bacteria. The CDC estimates about 80,000 vibriosis cases a year in the US, 52,000 of them from food, mostly raw or undercooked shellfish, especially oysters Q22. A contaminated oyster looks, smells and tastes normal.

Reported cases are far fewer. In 2024 there were 3,743 reported cases, 1,050 people hospitalised (32%) and 73 deaths (2%) Q23. An often-repeated figure of 100 deaths a year comes from an older estimate (flagged).

Vibrio vulnificus is the dangerous one. There are 150–200 reported infections a year, and about 1 in 5 people die, sometimes within 1–2 days. Infections in the eastern US rose eightfold from 1988 to 2018, and the range is moving north about 48 km a year. Most infections come from wounds; about 10% come from seafood Q22. In 2024 there were 222 cases; 88% were hospitalised and 18% died Q23.

Of 745 patients who had eaten a single seafood item, 36% had eaten oysters, and 87% of those ate them raw Q23.
