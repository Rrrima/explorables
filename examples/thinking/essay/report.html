Research report

Do large language models make people think worse?

The evidence behind Thinking Inside the Box. Compiled 19 October 2026 from three research tracks and 134 sources, read from the primary page or paper wherever one could be reached.

About this report

Each claim carries a tag such as C6, H24 or M12. The letter names the track and its source list: C for controlled studies and measurements, H for history, culture and adoption, M for the learning and cognitive science that explains the mechanism. Four markers show confidence:

  • Disputed other researchers have challenged the finding, or a replication failed.
  • Secondary the number comes from press or a summary because the paper itself could not be read.
  • Preprint not peer reviewed at the time of writing.
  • Retracted withdrawn by the journal; cited only so that readers recognise it.

The question is young. Almost everything in track C was published between 2023 and 2026, most of it in the last eighteen months, and the field has already produced one retraction and one widely misreported preprint. Where sources disagree, both are given and the essay says which it uses.

Track C

Studies: what has been measured

C1The MIT brain study

"Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna et al., MIT Media Lab) is a 216-page arXiv preprint, first posted in June 2025, marked "under review" on every page and not peer reviewed as of October 2026 C1 Preprint. Fifty-four people from five Boston universities, eighteen per group, wrote one SAT-style essay per session in twenty minutes, over three sessions, using ChatGPT, a search engine, or nothing. Eighteen came back for a fourth session in which the tools were swapped: nine ChatGPT writers wrote unaided and nine unaided writers used ChatGPT. A 32-channel EEG measured directed connectivity between scalp electrodes while they wrote.

Its clearest finding is behavioural. Minutes after finishing, 0 of 18 ChatGPT writers could produce a correct quotation from their own essay, against 15 of 18 search-engine writers and 16 of 18 unaided writers; 15 of 18 ChatGPT writers could not quote anything at all C1. By the third session six of the eighteen still could not. In session four, seven of the nine who moved from ChatGPT to no tools failed to quote, against one of nine going the other way. Ownership followed the same pattern: half the ChatGPT group claimed full ownership of their essay and three claimed none; no one in the other groups claimed none.

The EEG result is the one that travelled. The paper reports that connectivity "systematically scaled down with the amount of external support": the search-engine group showed about 34–48% lower total connectivity than the unaided group depending on frequency band, and the ChatGPT group "up to 55% reduced total dDTF magnitude" in the low-frequency bands the authors associate with semantic processing and monitoring C1. The 55% is a maximum over bands and regions, not an average. The essays of the ChatGPT group were also the most alike in their named entities, phrasing and topics. From this the authors coin "cognitive debt": "repeated reliance on external systems like LLMs replaces the effortful cognitive processes required for independent thinking."

C2Its critics, and its authors

A December 2025 commentary by Stanković and colleagues lists five concerns: sample size, reproducibility, the EEG analysis, inconsistencies in reporting, and transparency C2 Preprint. Their power calculation for the design says about 159 participants would be needed for adequate power, against 54 used and 18 in the crossover; some topic-level figures rest on two to four essays per group. Re-counting significant connections, they find the ChatGPT group showed greater beta-band engagement than the unaided group, "reducing the consistency of this finding", and they note that "lower connectivity does not necessarily indicate reduced cognitive engagement." The search-engine group, which also delegated, showed no impairment on the behavioural measures.

The authors largely agree about what the study is not. Their limitations section asks for larger, more diverse samples and notes that the results cannot be generalised beyond one model and one task. Their FAQ answers the question "Is it safe to say that LLMs are, in essence, making us 'dumber'?" with "No!", and asks journalists not to use the words "stupid", "dumb", "brain rot", "harm", "damage", "brain scans" or "terrifying findings" C3. TIME's headline on 17 June 2025 was "ChatGPT May Be Eroding Critical Thinking Skills, According to a New MIT Study" H43. The lead author told TIME she had skipped peer review because "I am afraid in 6-8 months, there will be some policymaker who decides, 'let's do GPT kindergarten.'"

The essay's reading: the EEG measures how much of a twenty-minute task a person did in their own head, during that task. It does not measure ability, lasting change, or damage. The quoting result is robust and unsurprising in a useful way: you do not remember what you did not write.

C3Surveys of knowledge workers

Lee and colleagues at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 first-hand examples of generative-AI use at work (CHI 2025, peer reviewed) C4. Workers said they exercised critical thinking in 59% of the examples, and most said the tool reduced the effort: 72% of examples at the "knowledge" level of Bloom's taxonomy, 79% at comprehension, 55% at evaluation. The regression result the essay uses: confidence that the AI can do the task predicts less enacted critical thinking (β = −0.69, p < 0.001), while confidence in doing the task oneself predicts more (β = 0.26) and confidence in one's ability to evaluate the AI's output predicts more still (β = 0.31). The authors describe a shift "from task execution to task stewardship", and warn that participants sometimes "conflated reduced effort in using GenAI with reduced effort in critical thinking". It is self-report and correlational.

Gerlich's survey of 666 people (Societies, 2025) found a negative correlation between frequent AI use and a critical-thinking score, mediated by self-reported offloading, and lower scores in younger participants C5. The widely quoted r values (−0.68, +0.72, −0.75) could only be read from press coverage C29 Secondary; the author himself says the study "highlights correlations rather than direct causation" and that the tools "are not inherently detrimental". The essay treats it as suggestive only.

C4Practice versus exam: the spine

Bastani and colleagues ran a pre-registered field experiment in a large Turkish high school, randomising about a thousand students by class across their in-class maths practice sessions (PNAS, June 2025, peer reviewed) C6. One arm practised with textbooks and notes, one with "GPT Base", a plain ChatGPT-style interface, and one with "GPT Tutor", the same model prompted with teacher-written hints and told not to give answers. Afterwards everyone sat a closed-book exam without the tool.

The paper's significance statement: "students relying on the technology may underperform when access to AI is subsequently removed, indicating reduced skill acquisition. However, … carefully designed safeguards, especially asking the AI tutor to provide teacher-designed hints instead of giving away answers, can mitigate these negative effects." Students in the Base arm often simply asked for the answer, and the model's own error rate on these problems was not trivial. Hamsa Bastani, in an interview: "if they start using these tools as a crutch and rely on it, then they won't actually build those fundamental skills" C28. The essay's "≈ 0" for the Tutor arm's exam is a reading of "largely mitigated"; the paper does not print a single number for it.

C5Other learning studies

  • Fan et al. 2025 (British Journal of Educational Technology): 117 university students randomised to ChatGPT, a human expert, a writing-analytics tool, or nothing, on an essay task. The ChatGPT group improved their essay scores most; their knowledge gain and transfer were no different from the others. The authors warn of "metacognitive laziness" C7.
  • Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024 (Computers in Human Behavior): 91 students researched nanoparticles in sunscreen with ChatGPT-3.5 or Google. The ChatGPT group reported lower cognitive load and produced lower-quality reasoning; the homogeneity of their recommendations did not differ, a null the essay keeps beside the sameness findings C8.
  • Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting 2025 (preprint; two pre-registered lab experiments and a field study): no overall effect of LLM access on learning, but using it to get solutions reduced understanding per topic (coefficient −0.31), while asking for explanations improved it (+0.25); access widened the gap between high and low prior-knowledge students C9 Preprint.
  • Kestin et al. 2025 (Scientific Reports): a Harvard introductory-physics randomised crossover with 194 students. A GPT-4 tutor with instructor-written, question-specific prompts and research-based pedagogy beat the same material taught in a well-run active-learning class: post-test medians 4.5 against 3.5 from a baseline of 2.75, an effect of 0.63 standard deviations by linear regression and 0.73–1.3 by quantile regression, in a median 49 minutes against a 60-minute class C10. The tutor was designed not to give answers away. The authors limit the claim to lower levels of Bloom's taxonomy and to a single lesson.
  • Melumad & Yun 2025 (PNAS Nexus; seven experiments, 10,462 participants): people who learned about a topic from an LLM, rather than by web search, spent less time, reported learning less, and then wrote shorter advice with fewer facts that blind readers rated less helpful; the LLM group's advice texts were far more alike (cosine similarity 0.159 against 0.057) C13. The effect held when the LLM was given live web links. This is the best-powered test of the "shallower knowledge" claim.

C6How people actually use it

Anthropic's Education Report (April 2025) classified 574,740 conversations from university accounts: students asked Claude to create in 39.8% of them and to analyse in 30.2%, with "remembering" at 1.8%; about 47% of conversations were direct requests for output and 53% collaborative C11. OpenAI's own study of about 1.1 million sampled conversations (NBER working paper, September 2025) puts non-work use above 70% of messages by mid-2025, writing at about a quarter of all messages and 40% of work messages, and tutoring at about a tenth; the company reported 700 million weekly users in July 2025 C12.

C7Work: productivity and calibration

On well-specified tasks the tool makes people faster and their output better. Noy and Zhang's pre-registered experiment with 453 professionals: writing time down 40%, graded quality up 18%, with the weakest writers gaining most C15. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's rollout to 5,179 customer-support agents: issues resolved per hour up 14%, up 34% for novices, with little effect on experienced agents C16. Peng and colleagues' Copilot experiment: a coding task finished 55.8% faster C20.

Two results complicate this. Dell'Acqua and colleagues gave 758 BCG consultants GPT-4 for eighteen realistic tasks inside the model's competence and one task outside it C17. Inside, quality rose (BCG's report says about 40%) and work was 25% faster; outside, consultants with the tool were about a fifth less likely to reach the correct answer (the HBS abstract says "19% less likely", an earlier version said 19 percentage points, BCG says "23% worse" C27 Disputed). Ed Mollick's write-up gives roughly 84% correct without AI against 60–70% with C30. BCG also found the AI group's ideas 41% less diverse.

And METR's randomised trial with sixteen experienced open-source developers on 246 real tasks in their own repositories found that tasks with AI took 19% longer (95% CI +2% to +39%), while the developers believed afterwards that they had been sped up by 20%, having forecast 24% C18. METR's February 2026 update with later tools and 57 developers moved to −4% to −18% in time, which it calls "only very weak evidence" of a speed-up C19. In Peng's study the mis-calibration ran the other way: developers guessed a 35% gain and got 56%. Self-perception is unreliable in both directions, and that is the point the essay takes from it.

C8Skill formation, and reasoning improved

Anthropic's Shen and Tamkin ran a randomised trial with 52 mostly junior software engineers learning an unfamiliar Python library, with or without an AI sidebar, followed by a comprehension quiz C21 Preprint. The AI group scored 50% against 67%, a 17-percentage-point gap (d = 0.74, p = 0.01), largest on debugging questions, and finished only about two minutes faster, not significantly. Those who delegated fully or leaned on the AI progressively scored under 40%; those who used it for conceptual questions scored 65% or more. "Cognitive effort—and even getting painfully stuck—is likely important for fostering mastery."

In the other direction, Costello, Pennycook and Rand had 2,190 conspiracy believers argue with GPT-4 Turbo for three rounds about their own evidence (Science, 2024): belief in the chosen conspiracy fell about 20%, the fall persisted undiminished for two months, and a fact-check of 128 of the model's claims found 99.2% true C22. Used as an interlocutor that argues with evidence, the same technology improved reasoning.

C9Sameness

Doshi and Hauser (Science Advances, 2024) had 293 writers produce eight-sentence stories with no AI help, one AI idea, or five; 600 evaluators judged them C14. Novelty rose 5.4% with one idea and 8.1% with five; usefulness 3.7% and 9.0%; the least creative writers gained most and reached parity with the most creative. But the stories within each AI condition became more alike by 10.7% and 8.9% of the similarity range, and 5% closer to the AI's own ideas: "writers are individually better off, but collectively a narrower scope of novel content is produced." With Melumad and Yun's advice texts, the MIT group's essays and BCG's consultants, four studies find convergence; Stadler's bounded scientific task did not.

C10Meta-analyses and their trouble

The most-cited pooled estimate of 2025, Wang and Fan's g = 0.867 across 51 studies, was retracted on 22 April 2026 for "discrepancies in the meta-analysis" C23 Retracted. Liu, Zuo and Lu's 37-study meta-analysis gives g = 0.577 (95% CI 0.395–0.759), larger in small samples, a warning sign C24. Deng and colleagues report positive effects without a figure the report could verify C25. Weidlich, Gašević, Drachsler and Kirschner audited the primary studies against three criteria (a described treatment, a described control, a valid measure of learning) and found only a small minority met all three; many measure performance while using ChatGPT, not learning afterwards, which is exactly the confound in the practice-versus-exam split C26.

Track H

History and culture: an old worry, and a new one by the numbers

H1Writing and print

"This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves… they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing." H1

King Thamus to Theuth, the inventor of letters, in Plato's Phaedrus (c. 370 BCE, Jowett's translation). The complaint survives because Plato wrote it down. In 1545 Conrad Gessner, cataloguing every known book, complained in his preface of "that confusing and harmful abundance of books" H3; in 1685 Adrien Baillet feared the multitude of books would make the coming centuries "as barbarous as that of the centuries that followed the fall of the Roman Empire". Trithemius's In Praise of Scribes (1492) argued that monks should keep copying by hand because paper would not last; he had it printed H4 Secondary.

H2Reading mania, radio, television, video games

Late eighteenth-century Germany diagnosed a Lesesucht, a reading mania; one 1794 essay was titled "Confidential letters on the present strange passion for reading and its influence on the decline in domestic and public happiness" H5. The telephone is the counter-example: a 2026 review of more than 40,000 articles from its first thirty years "found no evidence of panic" H8. In 1936 a magazine worried that children divided their attention between homework and the radio H7. On 9 May 1961 Newton Minow told broadcasters that if they watched their own station for a day, "what you will observe is a vast wasteland" H9. In 1982 the US Surgeon General warned of "aberrations in childhood behavior" from video games, then conceded he had "no scientific evidence" H10.

H3Calculators: the panic that was tested

In 1980 the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics recommended that programmes "take full advantage of the power of calculators and computers at all grade levels" H12, over sustained objection from teachers and parents. Hembree's meta-analysis of 79 studies and 529 effect sizes (dissertation 1984; with Dessart in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 1986) found that calculators used alongside ordinary instruction improved students' paper-and-pencil basic skills and problem solving at every grade except the fourth, where sustained use "appears to damage students' basic skills", and improved attitudes to mathematics H11. The 1986 article itself sits behind a paywall; the figures are from the dissertation it reports and a 1992 summary Secondary.

H4Google

Nicholas Carr's 2008 Atlantic essay "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" rehearsed the Phaedrus and the printing press before its own complaint: "Once I was a scuba diver in the sea of words. Now I zip along the surface like a guy on a Jet Ski" H16. Sparrow, Liu and Wegner's "Google effects on memory" (Science, 2011) reported that people who expected information to be saved recalled it less well and recalled where it was better: statements believed erased were recognised at 0.93 against 0.85–0.88 when saved H13. The paper's first experiment, a priming effect, failed to replicate in Camerer and colleagues' 2018 project, where 13 of 21 Nature and Science studies replicated at about half their original effect sizes H14, and again in a 2020 pre-registered attempt H15. The memory-for-location experiments were not part of those replications Disputed.

H5GPS, falling IQ scores, and "brain rot"

Dahmani and Bohbot tested fifty regular drivers: more lifetime GPS use went with worse spatial memory in self-guided navigation (r ≈ −0.20 to −0.26), and in thirteen people retested three years later, more GPS use in the interval went with steeper decline (r = −0.68) H17. GPS use was unrelated to people's self-rated sense of direction (r = 0.07), which the authors read as use causing the decline rather than the reverse. Small samples, the authors' own caveat.

IQ scores in Norway rose for cohorts born up to about 1975 and have fallen since. Bratsberg and Rogeberg (PNAS, 2018) showed both the rise and the fall inside families, between brothers, which rules out genes and immigration, and wrote that their analysis "does not speak to what these underlying environmental causes are" H18 H19. Dutton, van der Linden and Lynn's review found declines in seven countries, 2.4–3.2 points a decade on average, and favour explanations that Bratsberg and Rogeberg's design contradicts H20 Disputed. Neither camp blames screens.

Oxford's Word of the Year 2024 was "brain rot", its use up 230% in a year H21. The first recorded use is Thoreau's, in Walden (1854): "will not any endeavor to cure the brain-rot, which prevails so much more widely and fatally?" H22. He was blaming readers who wanted easy reading, not a machine.

H6Autopilot: the worry that was confirmed

Air France 447 fell into the Atlantic on 1 June 2009 after its autopilot disconnected. The French investigators' final report names among the causes "the absence of any training, at high altitude, in manual aeroplane handling" and "the total loss of cognitive control of the situation" H23. On 4 January 2013 the FAA issued a Safety Alert for Operators: "continuous use of those systems does not reinforce a pilot's knowledge and skills in manual flight operations … continuous use of autoflight systems could lead to degradation of the pilot's ability to quickly recover the aircraft from an undesired state" H24. Sixteen years earlier an American Airlines captain had called his colleagues "children of the magenta", after the guiding line on their screens; the AF447 captain had logged 346 hours in the previous six months, of which about four were hand-flying H25 Secondary.

H8Students

HEPI's surveys of about a thousand UK undergraduates each December: use of any generative-AI tool 66% (2024), 92% (2025), 95% (2026); use for assessed work 53%, 88%, 94%; AI-generated text pasted directly into assessed work 3%, 8%, 12% H30 H31. One student in the 2026 report: "I'm not using my brain at all." Pew: 26% of US teens used ChatGPT for schoolwork in 2024, double 2023 H32; by late 2025, 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork and one in ten said they do "all or most" of it with them H33. A global survey of 3,839 students in 2024 found 86% using AI in their studies H36.

H9Adults and the workplace

34% of US adults had used ChatGPT by early 2025, 58% of those under thirty H34. Gallup's survey of 23,068 US employees: 45% use AI at work at least a few times a year, 10% daily, 76% in technology and 33% in retail H37. Microsoft's self-interested but large Work Trend Index: 75% of knowledge workers say they use AI at work H38. Asked by Pew in June 2025, 53% of Americans expected AI to worsen people's ability to think creatively and 16% to improve it; the under-thirties were more pessimistic (61%) than the over-65s H35.

H10The public debate

New York City's schools blocked ChatGPT on 3 January 2023 because it "does not build critical-thinking and problem-solving skills" H44 and lifted the block on 18 May, the chancellor writing that "knee-jerk fear" had overlooked its potential H45. Anthropic's Learning mode (2 April 2025) "guides students' reasoning process rather than providing answers" H47; OpenAI's Study Mode (29 July 2025) asks questions to test understanding and may withhold direct answers, though students can switch it off H46. The MIT preprint's reception is in §C2. Pope Leo XIV, to young people in November 2025: "Use it in such a way that if it disappeared tomorrow, you would still know how to think, how to create, how to act on your own" H49.

Track M

Mechanisms: when offloading hurts and when it helps

M1Cognitive offloading

Risko and Gilbert define offloading as "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task in order to reduce cognitive demand" (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2016) M1. Offloading is usually rational and usually helps performance: people set reminders adaptively and do better for it M2; saving one file before studying another improves memory for the second (dz ≈ 0.93 in the original third experiment, by a later power analysis), because the first can be let go M3 M4. The theoretical warning is a loop: "The tendency to offload wayfinding to the more accurate GPS will likely reduce both our internal spatial memory for that location and our metacognitive confidence in it, which will in turn increase the likelihood that we choose to rely on the external artefact in the future … a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities." Searching the internet also inflates people's estimates of their own knowledge of unrelated topics, across nine experiments M8, an extension of the illusion of explanatory depth: people rate their understanding of zips and flush toilets highly until asked to explain them M9.

M2Effort and memory: desirable difficulties, generation, testing

Bjork and Bjork separate storage strength, how well something is learned, from retrieval strength, how accessible it is right now. Performance shows the second; learning is the first. "Conditions of learning that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support long-term retention and transfer, whereas conditions that create challenges and slow the rate of apparent learning often optimize long-term retention and transfer" M10. Generating an answer rather than reading it is one such difficulty: across 86 studies, d = 0.40 M11. Their caveat matters: a difficulty is desirable only when the learner can succeed at it.

Roediger and Karpicke's test-enhanced learning (2006) is the canonical demonstration M12. In their second experiment, 180 students read a passage four times, or three times and were tested once, or once and were tested three times. Five minutes later the re-readers did best (83% against 71% recalled). A week later the order had reversed: 40% for the re-readers, 61% for the thrice-tested. The re-readers had forgotten 52% of what they knew at five minutes; the tested group 14%. And the re-readers had predicted they would remember best. In the first experiment the same crossover: re-reading won at five minutes (81% to 75%) and lost at a week (42% to 56%).

Problem-solving before instruction ("productive failure") helps older learners on conceptual material (g = 0.36 across 53 studies) and reverses for young children and procedures M13. The popular claim that handwriting beats typing for notes did not survive direct replication; what survives is that verbatim transcription goes with worse learning M14 M16 Disputed.

M3When being given the answer helps

Cognitive load theory supplies the honest counterweight. Novices learning algebra from worked examples learned faster and made fewer errors than novices made to solve the same problems; searching for a solution overloads working memory and leaves nothing for building the pattern M17 Secondary. But the expertise reversal effect says the same guidance stops helping and starts hurting once the learner has the pattern: "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners" M18. Line-by-line glosses on Shakespeare helped novices and hurt experts. So an answer given helps a beginner on that task, and hurts the building of durable skill once a beginner is no longer one.

M4Automation, bias and skill decay

"A formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one. If he takes over he may set the process into oscillation." M21

Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (1983). Her final irony: "it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training." Parasuraman and Manzey's review of complacency and automation bias finds both in novices and experts, unfixed by training M19; Skitka, Mosier and Burdick found that people with a very-but-not-perfectly reliable aid monitored worse than people with none M20.

Skills decay with disuse, measurably. Arthur and colleagues' meta-analysis of 189 data points: loss grows from d = −0.01 immediately after training to d = −1.4 after more than a year, and cognitive tasks decay more (−1.15) than physical ones (−0.75) M22. A 2025 meta-analysis of procedural skills: about 0.08 standard deviations lost per month, half the training gain gone in about 6.5 months M23. Long-haul airline pilots, who hand-fly least, tracked a manual approach worst, and recent practice predicted skill better than total experience M25. And in four Polish endoscopy centres, after three months of AI-assisted colonoscopy, the doctors' adenoma detection rate in unassisted examinations fell from 28.4% (226 of 795) to 22.4% (145 of 648), a difference of 6.0 percentage points (95% CI −10.5 to −1.6), in an observational before-and-after design M24.

M5Knowing what you know

Kruger and Dunning's bottom-quartile performers scored at the 12th percentile and placed themselves at the 62nd; the skills needed to perform are the skills needed to judge performance M27. Fan's "metacognitive laziness" M28, Lee's confidence-in-AI result M29 and Roediger's confident re-readers are the same finding in three costumes: the feeling of fluency a tool produces is the feeling you would have had if you had learned it. Messeri and Crockett extend it to science: AI tools "exploit our cognitive limitations, making us vulnerable to illusions of understanding", risking "a phase of scientific enquiry in which we produce more but understand less" M30.

M6Tools as part of the mind, and the brain that practises

Clark and Chalmers' Otto keeps his memory in a notebook: "For Otto, his notebook plays the role usually played by a biological memory" M31. Their conditions for a tool to count as part of the mind include that its contents be automatically endorsed; a model whose output must be checked arguably fails that test. Hutchins moved "the boundaries of the cognitive unit of analysis out beyond the skin of the individual" to a ship's navigation team M32. Ong: "Technologies are not mere exterior aids but also interior transformations of consciousness", and "Plato's objections against writing are essentially the very same objections commonly urged today against computers" M33 M34.

The brain reallocates toward what it practises. Learning to read, even as an adult, reshapes the left fusiform cortex at a small cost to face processing M36. Of 79 London taxi trainees scanned before "the Knowledge", only the 39 who qualified grew posterior hippocampal grey matter, at a cost to other spatial memory; those who failed, and controls, did not change M39. Juggling practice changed grey matter within weeks and the change receded when practice stopped M40 M41. "Use it or lose it" is well supported for specific skills and not established for general cognitive ageing M42.

M7What good use looks like

Keep the effortful step with the person. The hint-only tutor in Turkey M43, the scaffolded Harvard tutor that would not give answers away M44, Lehmann's explanation-seekers M45, Danry's AI that framed its reasoning as questions and beat an always-correct explainer at improving logical discernment (n = 204) M46, and Buçinca's "cognitive forcing" designs that make people decide before they see the AI's answer, which reduced over-reliance and were disliked for it M47. One caution on tutors: Bloom's "2 sigma" came from two Chicago dissertations M49; across many more studies human tutoring averages d = 0.79 and step-based tutoring software 0.76 M50. Kestin's 0.63–1.3 is in the range of a good human tutor, not beyond it.

M8What the EEG can and cannot say

Alpha-band connectivity in this literature goes with internally directed attention and beta with active maintenance and control; more fronto-parietal connectivity while writing is read as more self-generated processing. It is a measure of engagement during one task, not of ability, consolidation or lasting change, and scalp EEG cannot see the hippocampus. Stanković and colleagues: "lower connectivity does not necessarily indicate reduced cognitive engagement. Variability … may indicate differences in attention shifts, changes in strategy, or simply lower motivation" M53.

M9The mechanism, assembled

Durable skill is built by the effort of retrieving, generating and struggling. A tool that hands over the answer raises today's performance while removing exactly the effort that would have built tomorrow's ability, and the loss is invisible from the inside, because the fluency the tool produces feels identical to the fluency of having learned. Whether offloading hurts depends on who (a novice without a pattern is helped; someone who has one is not), on what (disposable material can be released; needed skill cannot), and on whether the person will still be asked to judge the output or take over when the tool fails. The loop reinforces itself: less practice, less skill, less confidence, more reliance. Good use keeps the effortful step with the person and uses the tool to hint, question, argue and give feedback.

Open questions

  • No study yet follows LLM users for years. The longest horizons here are two months (Costello), four months (Kosmyna) and three years for GPS (thirteen people).
  • The MIT EEG findings await peer review; the critique's power calculation suggests the study is about a third of the size it would need to be.
  • Gerlich's correlation coefficients, Dell'Acqua's exact "19", the AF447 captain's four hand-flying hours and Hembree's 1986 article could only be read at second hand.
  • Bastani's Tutor arm has no printed exam number; "largely mitigated" is drawn as ≈ 0.
  • Whether the sameness effect holds on bounded factual tasks is open: four studies find convergence, Stadler's does not.
  • Which meta-analytic effect of ChatGPT on learning to believe, if any: 0.87 (retracted), 0.58, about 1.1, all with very high heterogeneity and mostly measuring performance during use.
  • Whether a questioning tutor's advantage survives beyond one lesson and the lower levels of Bloom's taxonomy.
