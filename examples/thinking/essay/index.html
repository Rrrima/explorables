An illustrated essay · with a research report

Thinking inside the box

Students who practised their maths with ChatGPT scored 48% better on the practice problems, and 17% worse on the exam that followed, once the tool was taken away C6.

Does a machine that thinks for you make you think worse? The honest answer is smaller and stranger than the headlines. This essay walks through the studies, the two-thousand-year history of the same worry, and the learning science that explains when handing over the effort costs you the skill, and when it doesn't.

I · History

Two and a half thousand years of worry

Every technology that holds memory for us has been accused of rotting it. One accusation was tested and failed. One was tested and confirmed. HoverTap each figure.

370 BCE

In Plato's Phaedrus, a king is shown the invention of writing and refuses it. It "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves" H1. The complaint survives because Plato wrote it down.

1545, and the 1790s

Printing brought "that confusing and harmful abundance of books" H3. By the end of the eighteenth century Germany had diagnosed a reading mania, and an essayist blamed it for "the decline in domestic and public happiness" H5. The telephone, oddly, caused no panic at all: forty thousand articles from its first thirty years contain none H8.

1961 to 1986: one worry gets tested

Television was "a vast wasteland" H9. Then calculators arrived in schools, and for once someone measured. Seventy-nine studies and 529 effect sizes later, children who used calculators alongside ordinary teaching had better paper-and-pencil skills, at every grade except the fourth H11.

2008 to 2020: two more, with different endings

"Is Google Making Us Stupid?" asked Nicholas Carr in 2008 H16. A 2011 study found people who expected information to be saved remembered it less, and where it was, more H13; its priming experiment did not replicate H14. Two worries did hold up. Drivers who used GPS more had worse spatial memory, and did not know it H17. And after Air France 447, the FAA warned in writing that "continuous use of autoflight systems could lead to degradation of the pilot's ability" to recover the aircraft H24.

2022: the cube

ChatGPT reached a hundred million users in two months H26 and nine hundred million a week by early 2026 H28. In the UK, 95 of every 100 undergraduates now use generative AI, 94 of them for assessed work H31; 54% of American teenagers use chatbots for schoolwork, and one in ten does all or most of it that way H33. The old worry has never had so many people to worry about.

II · The study everyone read about

A room with fifty-four people

The 2025 MIT preprint that put "cognitive debt" into the papers is smaller than its coverage. Its clearest finding is not in the brain scan.

Fifty-four people from five Boston universities, eighteen to a group, wrote one short essay a session for three sessions: with ChatGPT, with a search engine, or with nothing but their heads. Eighteen came back for a fourth session with the tools swapped. A cap of electrodes recorded their brains while they wrote C1.

Minutes after finishing, each was asked to quote a sentence from their own essay. In the first session, none of the eighteen ChatGPT writers could. Fifteen of the search-engine writers and sixteen of the unaided writers could C1. By the third session six of the ChatGPT group still could not. You do not remember what you did not write.

The brain result is the one that travelled: connectivity between regions "scaled down with the amount of external support", up to 55% lower in the ChatGPT group in the bands the authors tie to meaning and monitoring C1. The number is a maximum over bands and regions. What it measures is how much of a twenty-minute task a person did in their own head, during that task. It does not measure ability, lasting change, or damage M53.

A commentary calculated that the design needed about 159 participants to be adequately powered; it had 54, and 18 in the crossover C2. The authors' own FAQ answers "Is it safe to say that LLMs are making us dumber?" with "No!", and asks journalists not to write "stupid", "dumb", "brain rot", "harm" or "damage" C3. TIME's headline: "ChatGPT May Be Eroding Critical Thinking Skills" H43.

III · The spine

The practice room and the exam room

The cleanest experiment so far put a thousand high-school students in one room with the tool, then in another without it. The same tool had opposite effects in the two rooms.

The practice room

In a large Turkish high school, about a thousand students were randomised by class for their in-class maths practice C6. One group had textbooks. One had a plain ChatGPT-style interface. One had the same model told to give teacher-written hints and never the answer. With the tool, practice scores rose 48% with plain ChatGPT and 127% with the hinting tutor.

The exam room

Then everyone sat a closed-book exam without it. The plain-ChatGPT students scored 17% lower than the students who had never had access. The hint-only students scored about the same as the control: the guardrails, the paper says, "largely mitigated" the harm C6. Students had used the plain model, in the authors' word, as a crutch.

Seventeen again

Fifty-two junior programmers learned an unfamiliar Python library, half with an AI assistant, half by hand, then took a quiz C21. The assisted group finished about two minutes faster, not significantly, and scored 50% against 67%: seventeen points lower, worst on debugging. The ones who used the assistant to ask conceptual questions scored as well as anyone.

Product and learning come apart

The pattern repeats in smaller studies. Students writing with ChatGPT improved their essay scores most and learned no more than anyone else C7. Students researching with it felt less strain and reasoned worse C8. The work gets better. The person does not, unless something makes them do the work.

IV · Mechanism

Why effort is the point

Memory is built by the work of retrieving and generating, not by exposure to the answer. A tool that removes the work removes the building. Drag the days.

Two people learn the same passage. One reads it four times. The other reads it once and is made to write down what she remembers, three times, with no answer key M12. The first is handed the knowledge whole. The second has to build it.

Five minutes later

The reader wins: 83% recalled against 71%. She also predicts, with more confidence than anyone, that she will remember it best M12. This is the practice room. Fluency now feels exactly like learning.

A week later

The reader has 40%. The builder has 61% M12. The re-reader forgot half of what she knew at five minutes; the tested learner forgot a seventh. Across 86 studies, generating an answer rather than reading it is worth about four tenths of a standard deviation M11. "Conditions that create challenges and slow the rate of apparent learning often optimize long-term retention" M10.

And skills, once built, decay

A year without using a trained skill costs about 1.4 standard deviations, more for cognitive skills than physical ones M22. After three months of AI-assisted colonoscopy, doctors' detection rate in unassisted examinations fell from 28.4% to 22.4% M24. Lisanne Bainbridge saw it in 1983: "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one" M21.

V · Calibration

The mirror

The damage is hard to see from inside, because the tool's fluency feels like your own. People are wrong about what it does for them, in both directions.

Felt: 20% faster. Measured: 19% slower.

Sixteen experienced open-source developers were randomised, task by task, to use AI tools or not, on 246 real issues in code they had maintained for years C18. With AI the tasks took 19% longer. Afterwards the developers believed they had been 20% faster. A 2026 follow-up with newer tools found a small, uncertain speed-up C19; the gap between feeling and clock is the finding.

The other direction

Ninety-five developers writing a small web server with Copilot finished 56% faster and guessed they had gained 35% C20. Writers whose AI-assisted stories judges rated better did not rate them better themselves C14. Self-report is not a measurement of what the tool does to you.

Trust in the tool, less thinking

Among 319 knowledge workers describing 936 real uses, confidence that the AI could do the task predicted less critical thinking (β = −0.69). Confidence in their own ability to do it, and to judge the output, predicted more (β = 0.26 and 0.31) C4. Their work shifted, in the authors' phrase, "from task execution to task stewardship".

The edge of the tool's competence

Seven hundred and fifty-eight consultants used GPT-4 for tasks inside its competence and one task outside it C17. Inside, quality rose about 40%. Outside, where the model was wrong, those using it were about a fifth less likely to reach the right answer: roughly 84% correct without it, 60–70% with C30. The fifty GPS drivers were the same: heavier users navigated worse, yet rated their sense of direction no lower H17.

VI · Collective

Everyone writes the same essay

Individually the tool makes work a little better. Collectively it makes everyone's work more alike. Press the button under the drawing.

Nearly three hundred people each wrote an eight-sentence story; six hundred readers judged them C14. Each drop is one story, placed by what it is about. Some writers worked alone. Some were given one idea from GPT-4 to start from. Some were given five.

Each story got a little better: novelty up 8% and usefulness up 9% with five ideas, and the least creative writers gained most C14. But within each AI group the stories drew together, by about a tenth of the whole range of similarity, and closer to the AI's own ideas. "Writers are individually better off, but collectively a narrower scope of novel content is produced."

It repeats. People who learned a topic from an LLM rather than a search engine then wrote advice that was shorter, rated less helpful, and far more alike: a similarity of 0.16 against 0.06 C13. Consultants with GPT-4 produced 41% less diverse ideas C27. The ChatGPT essays in the MIT study were the most homogeneous of the three groups C1. One study of a bounded scientific question found no narrowing at all C8.

VII · What good use looks like

The tutor that will not answer

Make the tool hint, explain or argue instead of answering, and the harm disappears or reverses. Choose what she asks for.

Same model, two prompts. Asked for the answer, it hands over the cube: practice up 48%, the exam down 17%. Told to give the teacher's hints and nothing more, it hands over pieces: practice up 127%, and the exam about the same as never having used it C6.

At Harvard, a GPT-4 tutor built around instructor-written, question-specific prompts, told to scaffold and not to give answers away, was tested against a well-run active-learning class on the same physics. The tutor's students learned more, by about 0.7 to 1.3 standard deviations, in a median 49 minutes against 60 C10. That is the range of a good human tutor, whose average is about 0.8 M50, not beyond it.

Students who asked for explanations understood more (+0.25); students who asked for solutions covered more and understood less (−0.31) C9. And when 2,190 conspiracy believers argued with GPT-4 about their own evidence for three rounds, belief fell about 20% and stayed down two months later; 99% of the model's claims checked out C22. Used to argue rather than to answer, the same machine made people reason better.

The companies have noticed. Anthropic's Learning mode "guides students' reasoning process rather than providing answers" H47; OpenAI's Study Mode asks questions back and may withhold the answer, though students can switch it off H46. Designs that force people to decide before seeing the AI's answer reduce over-reliance, and people dislike them for it M47. The effortful step has to stay with the person.

Coda

Not dumber. Worse at the thing you let the tool do for you, for as long as you let it, and unable to feel the difference. The evidence is young, small and loud, but it agrees on that much.

The test, offered to a hall of teenagers in November 2025: "Use it in such a way that if it disappeared tomorrow, you would still know how to think" H49.
